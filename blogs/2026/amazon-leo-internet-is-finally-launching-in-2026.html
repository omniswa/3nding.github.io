Amazon Leo Internet Is Finally Launching in 2026

July 4, 2026 7-Minute Read

Amazon just crossed the threshold it’s been chasing for three years, and almost nobody outside the industry noticed. On July 2, 2026, a United Launch Alliance rocket carried 29 more satellites into orbit, pushing Amazon Leo’s constellation past 390 units. That number matters because Amazon’s own engineers set it as the minimum needed to run continuous service across a first band of latitudes. The company said it now has enough satellites in orbit to begin “initial service” of its Leo internet-from-space network later this year.

So the direct answer, if you came here just for that: Amazon Leo will start switching on real customers before the end of 2026, but only in a narrow slice of the globe, and Amazon still hasn’t published a price.

Why “Enough Satellites” Doesn’t Mean “Everywhere”

A satellite internet constellation doesn’t work like flipping a switch. It works more like laying pipe across a country one section at a time, except the pipe is thousands of miles up and moving at 17,000 miles an hour.

Amazon plans a geographically limited initial rollout, focusing on higher latitudes near the north and south poles or mid-northern and mid-southern bands before expanding toward the equator. That’s not an accident of geography. Fewer satellites are needed to cover a narrow band far from the equator than to blanket the wider swath near it, so Amazon picks the cheapest coverage first and builds outward from there.

That explains why 390 satellites can support “initial service” while the full plan calls for far more. Amazon Leo’s constellation is designed to eventually hold roughly 3,236 satellites across three orbital shells. Three hundred ninety is a beachhead, not the finished network.

The Rebrand Nobody Asked For, But One That Actually Makes Sense

If you remember this project as Project Kuiper, you’re not behind. The service was rebranded as Amazon Leo in November 2025. The new name points directly at the physics doing the work: low Earth orbit, or LEO, the same approach Starlink uses to keep latency low.

Names rarely matter this much, but this one does a little bit of marketing labor for free. “Kuiper” referenced a belt of icy debris at the edge of the solar system. Nobody buying home internet cares about the Kuiper belt. “Leo” at least hints at something orbital and fast, which is the actual selling point.

What Amazon Has Actually Shipped So Far

Hardware exists, and that’s worth taking seriously, because plenty of satellite internet promises die at the prototype stage.

Amazon has announced three customer terminals, each aimed at a different kind of user:

  • Leo Nano — a compact antenna rated for speeds up to 100 Mbps, built for individual households with modest needs.
  • Leo Pro — a mid-size unit supporting up to 400 Mbps, likely the default option for most residential customers.
  • Leo Ultra — a much larger antenna capable of 1 Gbps downloads, aimed at businesses and heavy-use locations rather than a typical home.

In May 2026, an FCC filing revealed the router that will pair with these dishes. It’s small, roughly six inches square, with just two Ethernet ports and a power input. Simplicity here isn’t a design flourish. Fewer ports and a phone-app setup process mean fewer support calls, and fewer support calls mean Amazon can scale customer onboarding without hiring an army of technicians.

The Regulatory Clock Amazon Almost Missed

Here’s the part of the story that explains the urgency behind that July 2 launch. The FCC’s original license required Amazon to get half of its first-generation constellation into orbit by July 30, 2026, or risk losing priority spectrum rights.

Amazon didn’t hit that pace on its own timeline. Rocket availability, not satellite manufacturing, turned out to be the bottleneck; Amazon can build five satellites a day at its Kirkland factory, but building a satellite is pointless without a rocket to carry it. In June 2026, the FCC waived the deadline for launching half of the constellation, but said it would temporarily demote the spectral priority of satellites launched after the deadline until Amazon builds out the constellation at a faster pace.

That’s a soft penalty rather than a hard wall, and it explains why Amazon has been racing to stack up launches through the first half of the year. Losing spectrum priority doesn’t shut the network down, but it does mean Amazon’s signals could get bumped by competitors’ in shared frequency bands. Nobody wants to build a $10 billion network and then get told to wait in line.

Starlink Isn’t Standing Still, and That’s the Real Competitive Story

Comparing Amazon Leo to Starlink feels obvious, but the scale gap is worth stating plainly because it reframes what “launch” even means here. Amazon positions Leo as a direct competitor to SpaceX’s Starlink, which operates more than 10,000 satellites and serves about 12 million subscribers.

Amazon is entering with roughly 4% of Starlink’s satellite count. That’s a wide gap, and it means Amazon’s 2026 launch will look and feel small next to an incumbent that already blankets most of the inhabited world.

But small doesn’t mean irrelevant. Starlink already dropped its promotional first-month price to $59 for new subscribers heading into 2026, which is the kind of move companies make when they sense a competitor closing in. Competition doesn’t need equal scale to change pricing behavior. It just needs a credible alternative on the horizon.

Where Service Will Actually Show Up First

Five countries have been named as the initial target list: the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, France, and Germany. Amazon hasn’t attached specific dates or regions within those countries, which is a meaningfully different thing from a launch date.

An enterprise preview program has been running since November 2025, letting select business customers test the network before anyone else. This is a standard playbook for infrastructure companies. Business customers tolerate rough edges in exchange for early access and better support, and their feedback surfaces problems before millions of consumers ever see them.

Consumer sign-ups are the part still missing. As of this writing, Amazon has not opened a public waitlist for residential service, and pricing remains unannounced beyond a vague promise from CEO Andy Jassy that Leo will cost less than existing alternatives.

What This Means If You’re Actually Waiting for Service

If you live in a rural area with poor broadband options, the honest advice is to not put off a decision on Amazon Leo’s account, at least not yet. Starlink already works today, and Amazon has given no signal that residential sign-ups open before late 2026 at the earliest, with even that timeline resting on continued launch success.

If you’re weighing this as a longer-term bet, the calculus changes. Amazon’s manufacturing capacity, its logistics network built for shipping literally everything to everyone, and its existing AWS ground station infrastructure are real structural advantages that Starlink didn’t have when it started. A company that already knows how to get a package to a rural doorstep in two days has a genuine edge in getting satellite dishes into the hands of exactly the customers who need them most.

The satellites are up. The hardware is designed. The regulatory clock has been reset rather than stopped. What’s left is the unglamorous part: raising hundreds of satellites to their operating altitude, expanding coverage band by band, and eventually telling the public what this is going to cost. That last piece is the one actually standing between Amazon Leo and your router.
