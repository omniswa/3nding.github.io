Cloudflare Cuts 1,100 Jobs at Its Revenue Peak

May 9, 2026 10-Minute Read

On May 7, 2026, Cloudflare posted the best quarter in its 16-year history. Revenue hit $639.8 million, up 34% year-over-year — the highest single quarter the company has ever recorded. [BitcoinWorld] Then, in the same earnings release, it announced it was eliminating 1,100 jobs. One in five employees. Gone.

CEO Matthew Prince called it something “we’ve never done in Cloudflare’s history.” [TechCrunch] That admission matters. This wasn’t a struggling company trimming fat. It was a thriving company making a deliberate architectural decision — and framing that decision entirely around artificial intelligence.

What follows is an attempt to read that decision clearly, without the cheerleading or the panic that has surrounded it.

What Actually Happened

On May 7, 2026, co-founders Matthew Prince and Michelle Zatlyn published a blog post titled “Building for the Future,” announcing a global workforce reduction of more than 1,100 employees. The cuts represent roughly one in five Cloudflare employees, taking the company from a reported 5,156 headcount down to approximately 4,000. [Layoffhedge]

The company cut people from all teams and geographies, with one explicit exception: salespeople carrying revenue quotas were retained. [TechCrunch] That carve-out is telling. The jobs being eliminated weren’t the ones closest to customers or revenue generation — they were support roles, coordination roles, the operational scaffolding that held things together when humans did the work.

In their blog post, Prince and Zatlyn wrote: “Today’s actions are not a cost-cutting exercise or an assessment of individuals’ performance; they are about Cloudflare defining how a world-class, high-growth company operates and creates value in the agentic AI era.” [TechCrunch]

Whether that framing is a fair description of events, or a rebranding of a cost-cutting exercise, is genuinely debatable. The honest answer is probably somewhere between the two.

The 600% Number and What It’s Actually Claiming

The most striking figure in Cloudflare’s announcement wasn’t the headcount reduction. It was the claim that Cloudflare’s internal use of AI had increased by more than 600% in the last three months alone. [The Register]

Prince elaborated on what that looks like internally. Virtually the entire R&D team now uses the company’s Workers platform for AI-powered coding. One hundred percent of code produced this way and deployed in Cloudflare’s products is now reviewed by autonomous AI agents. Employees across engineering, HR, finance, and marketing run thousands of AI agent sessions daily. [BitcoinWorld]

Prince described the tipping point as last November: “Across our teams, we began to see massive productivity gains — team members that were two, 10, even 100 times more productive than they had been before. It was like going from a manual to an electric screwdriver.” [TechCrunch]

The electric screwdriver analogy is vivid, but it raises an obvious question: if your tools got dramatically faster, why are you building less? Cloudflare’s answer is that more productive engineers need fewer people around them — fewer coordinators, fewer reviewers, fewer support layers. The organization chart that made sense for a thousand engineers doesn’t make sense for five hundred engineers who each produce twice the output.

That logic is coherent. It is also, conveniently, untestable until 2027.

The Market’s Reaction Wasn’t About the Financials

Cloudflare shares sank 24% the day after the announcement. [CNBC] Not because the earnings were bad. They weren’t.

By every conventional measure, Q1 was excellent. Revenue grew 34% year over year. Non-GAAP gross margin held at 73%. The company added a record number of large customers — those paying more than $100,000 annually. [Let’s Data Science] It also saw a 73% year-over-year increase in deals worth more than a million dollars. [The Next Web]

The market did not punish Cloudflare for the financials. It punished Cloudflare for the message. If a company that just delivered 34% revenue growth concludes that a fifth of its workforce is no longer needed because of agents it deployed in the past 90 days, every other software vendor will be expected to deliver the same story by the next earnings cycle. [Let’s Data Science]

That’s the actual dynamic at play. Investors weren’t selling because Cloudflare looked weak. They were selling because Cloudflare, inadvertently, made every other tech company look bloated.

Prince’s Q2 revenue guidance topped out at $665 million against a consensus estimate of $666.1 million. The miss was small. The reaction was not. [Let’s Data Science] The real source of volatility was what the memo signaled about the industry, not what it said about the quarter.

“Not a Cost-Cutting Exercise” — How True Is That?

Cloudflare expects to take restructuring charges of between $140 million and $150 million related to the layoffs, largely in Q2. [The American Bazaar] The severance packages, by most accounts, are exceptional.

Affected employees will continue to be paid through the end of the year, receive healthcare benefits through the end of 2026 for U.S.-based employees, and have their equity continue to vest through August 15. [Yahoo Finance] In practical terms: roughly eight months of salary, eight months of insurance, and a few extra months of stock accumulation for more than 1,000 people.

When Snap announced 1,000 layoffs recently, it offered four months of severance — considered generous by sector standards, but still half of what Cloudflare is providing. [Yahoo Finance]

The generous severance is real. But so is the math underneath it. Cloudflare narrowed its net loss to $22.9 million in Q1 2026, down from $38.5 million in the same quarter last year. [H2S Media] A company that achieves long-term headcount reductions — even after an expensive severance bill — will eventually benefit structurally. The cost savings are deferred, but they’re still there.

Saying the layoffs aren’t about costs isn’t dishonest. It’s just incomplete.

The Specific Jobs That AI Is Replacing

One non-obvious detail buried in the announcement: the layoffs weren’t primarily targeting engineers. Prince was explicit that “a lot of the support roles” behind customer-facing and code-creating employees “are not going to be the roles that drive companies going forward.” [The Register]

This matters because it runs counter to the popular narrative that AI primarily threatens knowledge workers writing code or content. The first real wave of AI-driven displacement inside a high-growth tech company is hitting coordination, support infrastructure, and middle-layer roles — the people whose job was to make other people’s jobs run smoothly.

Cloudflare’s announcement is notable because it was the most explicit yet in attributing layoffs to AI replacing human work, rather than AI requiring capital reallocation. Meta’s Zuckerberg said the layoffs were about freeing capital for AI infrastructure. Cloudflare’s Prince said the layoffs were about AI doing the work. [The Next Web]

That distinction is sharper than it sounds. One framing says “we need money to buy GPUs.” The other says “we no longer need you because the agents are handling it.” Cloudflare is the first major tech company to commit fully to the second argument in a public earnings call.

A 16-Year Record Broken at the Best Possible Moment

This was the first mass layoff in Cloudflare’s 16-year history. [TechCrunch] The timing — announced alongside a record quarter — is either an act of unusual transparency or a calculated softening of the blow. Possibly both.

Cloudflare now has 4,416 customers paying more than $100,000 per year. It estimates that approximately 80% of leading AI companies use its products. Its Workers developer platform, which runs code at the edge of Cloudflare’s network across data centers in 330 cities, is positioned as infrastructure for the AI agent economy the company says is replacing the roles it just eliminated. [The Next Web]

There’s a particular irony in the fact that Cloudflare’s own infrastructure is helping to power the agent economy that made 1,100 of its employees redundant. The company is both the builder and the early casualty of the thing it’s building.

What Prince Says Comes Next

Prince said Cloudflare will continue to hire — specifically people who embrace AI tools — and predicted that headcount in 2027 will exceed any point in 2026. [TechCrunch]

That’s a meaningful commitment if it holds. It would mean the current reduction is genuinely a transition, not a permanent contraction. It would also mean Cloudflare plans to add back different kinds of roles — people who work alongside agents rather than roles that agents have made redundant.

Full-year 2026 revenue guidance came in at $2.805 to $2.813 billion, narrowly beating Wall Street’s estimate of $2.8 billion. Full-year adjusted earnings are expected at $1.19 to $1.20 per share, ahead of the $1.14 expected. [CNBC]

The financial trajectory suggests growth isn’t slowing. Whether that growth will translate into the hiring Prince is promising, or whether the company continues to extract more output from fewer people, is the question that won’t be answered until mid-2027.

The Broader Pattern — and Why Cloudflare Is the Clearest Case Yet

The tech sector recorded more than 73,000 job cuts across 95 companies in the first four months of 2026, with projections that the full-year total will exceed the 124,000 eliminated in all of 2025. [The Next Web]

Cloudflare joined a growing list of tech companies — including Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon — that have reported increased revenue alongside massive layoffs, attributing both trends to AI. [TechCrunch] But Cloudflare’s version of this story is the most candid. It published specific internal AI usage numbers and tied them directly to headcount decisions, on a public earnings call, with the founders’ names on the blog post.

That transparency has a cost. Every CFO at every software company is now being asked the same question their analyst probably texted them Thursday night: If Cloudflare can grow 34% with 20% fewer people, why can’t we?

The sequence is becoming the template for the technology industry in 2026: record revenue, record layoffs, record doubt about what comes next. [Let’s Data Science]

The Test That’s Still Coming

The real verdict on Cloudflare’s decision won’t arrive in an earnings call. It’ll arrive in how the company performs through the operational disruption of removing one in five employees. Cultural memory, institutional knowledge, the informal networks that make any organization actually function — none of those show up in a productivity metric, and none of them survive a 20% headcount cut without some damage.

If Cloudflare’s revenue growth rate decelerates after Q3 alongside the headcount cut, the agentic AI thesis as told to public-company shareholders will face its first real test. [The Next Web]

If the growth holds — or accelerates — Prince will have made one of the most significant organizational bets in tech company history and won it. The playbook he’s writing right now would become the standard template within eighteen months.

If it doesn’t hold, the 1,100 people who lost their jobs during Cloudflare’s best quarter will have been the cost of an experiment that didn’t work.

The earnings beat. The cuts are done. The agents are running. What happens next is genuinely unknown — and that uncertainty, more than anything else in the announcement, is why the stock fell 24% the morning after the best quarter Cloudflare ever had.
