AI Factories Are Splitting the Power Equipment Market in Two

July 3, 2026 7-Minute Read

Nobody builds an AI data center and gets stuck waiting on the chips. That part is almost boring now — Nvidia ships, the racks arrive, the GPUs go in. The thing that actually stalls a project in 2026 is a piece of equipment most people have never thought about: the transformer.

So here’s the direct answer. The winners in this cycle are the handful of companies that make gas turbines, transformers, and switchgear — GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi Power, Eaton, and Vertiv chief among them. The losers are everyone downstream of a queue: developers without equipment locked in, small utilities competing against hyperscalers for the same factory slots, and, increasingly, ordinary electricity ratepayers. This isn’t a chip story anymore. It’s a copper-and-steel story, and copper and steel don’t scale the way software does.

Why a Data Center Is Really a Power Plant With Servers Attached

Big Tech will spend more than $650 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026, and that number gets most of the headlines. But spending money and taking delivery of equipment are two different problems.

Roughly 12 to 16 gigawatts of U.S. data center capacity was slated to come online this year, yet only about a third of it is actually under construction. The rest is stuck. Not because the land isn’t ready, and not because the financing fell through. It’s stuck because grid transformer backlogs stretch toward five years in some categories, and a data center that can’t get power isn’t a data center. It’s an expensive, empty warehouse.

That’s the counter-intuitive part. Electrical equipment is a tiny slice of a data center’s total cost — under 10 percent, by most estimates. And yet it decides everything. One missing transformer can freeze a billion-dollar project the same way one missing bolt can ground a plane.

The Transformer Chokepoint Nobody Saw Coming

Demand for the transformers that step generation up to the grid — the generator step-up units — grew roughly 274 percent between 2019 and 2025, according to Wood Mackenzie. Substation transformer demand rose about 116 percent over the same stretch. Nobody built factories fast enough to match that curve, because nobody was modeling a curve like that.

Lead times for the largest units have stretched to as long as four years, according to PwC analysts. Pre-2020, that same order took roughly two years. The materials matter too: these things need grain-oriented electrical steel and copper windings, and both are now fought over by data centers, EV makers, and utilities replacing aging equipment all at once.

Here’s the uncomfortable part. U.S. imports of Chinese high-power transformers jumped from fewer than 1,500 units in 2022 to more than 8,000 units through October 2025, per Wood Mackenzie data. China still controls somewhere around 60 percent of global transformer production capacity. So the AI buildout in America runs, in part, through Chinese factories. That’s not a political statement. It’s just where the steel comes from right now.

The Winners: Whoever Locked In Orders Before Everyone Else Panicked

GE Vernova turned into the clearest proof that grid hardware, not chips, is the real AI trade. Orders surged 71 percent year-over-year to $18.3 billion in the first quarter of 2026, pushing its backlog to $163 billion. Its gas turbine business is booked so far out that the CEO expects reservations to be sold out through 2030 by the end of 2026. Customers aren’t just placing orders anymore. They’re paying to reserve a spot in line.

Eaton sits closer to the building itself, in switchgear and distribution. Data center orders were up roughly 240 percent year-over-year in the first quarter of 2026, with that segment’s backlog reaching $22.8 billion. That’s not organic growth. That’s a company that happened to already own the factories everyone suddenly needs.

Vertiv grew sales about 30 percent in the first quarter of 2026, with operating profit up over 50 percent, and raised full-year guidance to as much as $14 billion. It’s one of the only large players covering both power and cooling under one roof, which matters because AI racks generate heat nobody designed 2019-era data centers to handle.

Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi Power round out the turbine oligopoly. Siemens now carries a backlog north of €136 billion, the largest in its history, with the majority of its 2025 gas turbine orders tracing back to data center demand. Mitsubishi’s delivery slots are mostly gone through 2027 and 2028, even as it races to double capacity.

The logic connecting all five companies is the same. Manufacturing heavy electrical equipment takes years to scale because it requires specialized factories, skilled welders, and raw materials that don’t magically appear. Software can scale in a weekend. A turbine factory cannot.

The Losers Are Quieter, But There Are More of Them

Some losses are obvious. Developers who didn’t lock in transformer orders three or four years ago are now watching competitors leapfrog them, because the winners in this regime are whoever secured power purchase agreements and equipment orders before anyone modeled today’s demand. Land, zoning, and tenant interest mean nothing if the energization date can’t be met.

Other losses are harder to see until the bill arrives. Utilities requested roughly $31 billion in rate increases across all of 2025 — more than double the amount sought the year before — with billions more filed in the first quarter of 2026 alone. One Virginia resident watched his monthly electric bill jump from around $100 to $281 in a single month. Surveys show most residents near major data center clusters believe those facilities are driving their bills higher.

Whether that belief matches reality is genuinely contested, and it’s worth sitting with the nuance rather than picking a side. Wholesale power prices near some data center clusters have risen sharply over five years, but that figure describes what utilities pay generators, not what households pay monthly — the two get conflated constantly in political debate. Some research has found no statistically significant link between data center concentration and current electricity prices at the state level. Other analysis of the PJM grid region attributes roughly half of recent capacity price increases to load growth, with the rest coming from market design changes and power plant retirements. Both things can be true at once: data centers are a real driver in specific pockets of the country, and they aren’t the whole story anywhere.

Small utilities and rural cooperatives lose in a different way. They’re bidding for the same factory slots as Google and Meta, with a fraction of the purchasing power and none of the leverage to jump the queue. A hyperscaler can pre-pay years out to reserve a manufacturing slot. A regional utility usually can’t.

The Workaround Economy: Skipping the Grid Entirely

When the grid itself becomes the bottleneck, some developers just route around it. The industry calls this bring-your-own-power, or BYOP, and it means generating electricity on-site rather than waiting years for a substation upgrade.

Fuel cells are one version of this. They produce power at low voltage directly, which sidesteps the need for the very high-voltage transformers carrying the longest lead times. Onsite gas turbines are another version, though those run into the same GE Vernova and Siemens backlogs everyone else is fighting through.

It’s a workaround, not a fix. It solves one developer’s problem while doing nothing for the broader grid, and in some cases it deepens the divide between companies with capital to build private power islands and everyone else still waiting on the utility.

What This Actually Means Going Forward

The equipment makers investing in new U.S. capacity now — including Hitachi Energy’s billion-dollar-plus commitment to a new plant and Siemens’ $421 million transformer factory in North Carolina — won’t fix the shortage this year or next. Those plants come online in 2027 and 2028. Anyone betting on a quick resolution is betting on physics moving faster than it actually does.

The honest takeaway is this: in an AI buildout, the constraint was never going to be the flashy part. It was always going to be the boring, heavy, unglamorous machinery that nobody photographs for a product launch. Whoever owns that machinery owns the leverage. Everyone else is in line.
