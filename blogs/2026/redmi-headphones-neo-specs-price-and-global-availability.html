Redmi Headphones Neo: Specs, Price, and Global Availability

May 30, 2026 8-Minute Read

Xiaomi doesn’t usually make a big show of entering a new category. The Redmi Headphones Neo — the brand’s first proper over-ear wireless headphones — arrived in Japan quietly in late May 2026, then spread across Southeast Asia, Europe, and Australia within days. No splashy keynote. No countdown timer. Just retail listings going live in a dozen markets almost simultaneously, with prices that immediately made the competition look like it was charging a premium for nothing in particular.

That’s worth paying attention to.

What Xiaomi is actually launching here

The Redmi Headphones Neo use 40mm titanium-plated drivers and support Hi-Res Audio when connected via cable. Bluetooth playback runs on Bluetooth 5.4, and users can switch between two paired devices without reconnecting each time. [Gizmochina]

The Bluetooth-to-wired distinction is more meaningful than it sounds. In Bluetooth mode, frequency response sits at 20Hz–20kHz. Switch to USB-C and it extends to 20Hz–40kHz. [Stuff South Africa] That upper ceiling of 40kHz is technically beyond human hearing — the ear tops out around 20kHz — but what it signals in practice is that the drivers aren’t the limiting factor. Hi-Res certification via USB-C is legitimate here, not marketing decoration.

For situations where latency matters — gaming, video editing, real-time audio — the USB Type-C audio path is there. [Gizmochina] Most headphones in this segment skip wired entirely. The Neo doesn’t, which matters more than the spec sheet suggests, because wired audio eliminates Bluetooth codec variability entirely. Whatever quality the source has, you hear it.

The ANC number and what it’s competing against

The headphones claim up to 42dB of active noise cancellation, backed by a three-microphone system that filters background noise during calls. [Gizmochina]

42dB is a specific, concrete figure — and it’s being quoted in an over-ear form factor at roughly $55–$66 depending on market. For context, Honor’s Earbuds 4 launched around the same week claiming the same 42dB ANC depth at around $100. [Gizchina] Those are in-ears. Getting equivalent noise reduction in an over-ear design that also benefits from passive isolation through the earcup seal — that’s a different proposition entirely, and potentially a more effective one in practice.

The honest caveat: ANC depth figures are tested in lab conditions at specific frequency ranges. Xiaomi’s own product page notes that the 42dB figure applies within a particular frequency range, and actual results vary by usage conditions. [MI] That’s true of every ANC headphone on the market. The number is a directional indicator, not a promise. What matters is how it performs on a commute or in a café, and that takes real-world use to verify.

Battery life: where the specs get genuinely interesting

The 600mAh battery is rated for up to 72 hours of playback with ANC disabled. A quick 10-minute charge is claimed to deliver around five hours of listening time. [Gizmochina]

72 hours without ANC is a notable figure. Even cut in half with noise cancellation running — which is a reasonable working estimate — that’s still 35+ hours, well above what Sony’s WH-CH720N offers at twice the price. The fast-charge claim of five hours from ten minutes of charging is more practically useful; it covers the “forgot to charge it last night” scenario that kills an otherwise good pair of headphones.

The 600mAh cell is on the larger side for this form factor. That explains the weight: at 263 grams, these are not featherweights. [Ubergizmo] That’s comparable to the Sony WH-1000XM5 at 250g, though most people in this price range aren’t cross-shopping flagship Sony. Whether the earcup design distributes that weight comfortably is something a spec sheet won’t tell you.

Global pricing by market

The Redmi Headphones Neo retails for RM 239 in Malaysia, S$65.90 in Singapore, THB 1,990 in Thailand, AUD 109 in Australia, KRW 72,800 in South Korea, and SEK 799 in Sweden. [Gizmochina]

In Japan, where the headphones launched first, the retail price is JPY 7,980 — roughly $50 — though they’ve already been spotted selling for JPY 6,980 (~$44). [Notebookcheck]

The regional spread is worth noting. Xiaomi isn’t pricing these uniformly — the Australian price converts to roughly $67 USD while the Singapore listing comes in around $49 USD. That’s typical of how Xiaomi calibrates to local market conditions, and it means checking the official regional Mi store before assuming any single price point is universal.

The rollout isn’t as global as it looks

Here’s the complication: this isn’t a clean simultaneous global launch. Both Obsidian Black and Sand White are available at launch, with a Mist Blue variant expected in September 2026. [Notebookcheck] That’s a four-month stagger on a colorway, which suggests either supply constraints or a deliberate phased approach — the kind of thing that creates regional availability gaps that frustrate buyers who want something specific.

The headphones are now listed across parts of Europe, Australia, East Asia, and Southeast Asia. [Gizmochina] North America is conspicuously absent from that list. Xiaomi’s retail presence in the US market has never fully materialized, and there’s no current indication these will appear on Amazon.com or at US retail. For buyers in those markets, grey-market imports from the Singapore or Malaysian listings are likely to be the practical path — with the usual caveats about warranty coverage and Fast Pair compatibility on non-regional Android distributions.

A few things worth understanding before assuming this is the best budget ANC headphone

The Redmi Headphones Neo sits in a segment that’s genuinely crowded. Above true budget options like the Anker Q20+ but below Sony’s WH-CH720N at around $100, the Neo is competing with experienced players who’ve had years to tune both hardware and software. [Gizchina]

Xiaomi’s track record with earbuds — the Redmi Buds 8 series — has been solid on value. Over-ear headphones are a harder problem. Earcup resonance, headband pressure distribution, and long-session comfort all involve physical engineering tradeoffs that aren’t visible in a spec sheet. The Buds 8 Pro could lean on a small, refined form factor. The Headphones Neo has to hold up across a larger surface area with more contact points.

The Australian product page mentions wind noise cancellation during calls at wind speeds up to 5m/s and a dual-device connection that lets two pairs of headphones share audio from a single device via HyperOS. [MI] The audio sharing feature is genuinely useful for watching something with a partner on a plane. But it requires both people to have Redmi Headphones Neo — a narrow use case that’s more of a nice-to-have than a selling point for most buyers.

The EQ situation is worth mentioning too. Five presets in the Xiaomi Earbuds app is functional but minimal. If sound signature customization matters to you, five presets is a starting point, not a toolkit. Competitors like Sony offer parametric EQ on their app. At this price, that’s probably an unfair comparison — but it’s a real tradeoff if the default tuning doesn’t suit how you listen.

What the USB-C audio support actually enables

This is the detail that most coverage has mentioned in passing without explaining why it’s meaningful.

Bluetooth audio involves encoding, transmitting, and decoding audio — a process that introduces both latency and codec-dependent compression. Even the best Bluetooth codecs (LDAC, aptX Lossless) add some processing. USB audio bypasses all of that. The signal goes from source to driver directly, which is why lossless audio formats are supported when connected via USB-C. [Stuff South Africa]

For music listening, the audible difference between a good Bluetooth codec and USB is subtle. For latency-sensitive tasks — lip-sync accuracy in video, timing in gaming — it’s not subtle at all. The inclusion of USB-C audio at this price point isn’t just a backup connection option. It’s a genuine functional upgrade for a meaningful subset of users, particularly anyone who edits video or plays games on a laptop and finds Bluetooth latency annoying.

The Redmi Headphones Neo is the product category answer to a question Xiaomi’s earbuds couldn’t address: what happens when someone wants over-ear comfort, genuine ANC, and a battery that lasts through a long-haul flight without paying Sony prices? On paper, the answer is compelling. The real test is whether Xiaomi’s first over-ear headphone can hold up when someone wears it for four hours on a train, or whether “entry-level” reasserts itself in the ways that a spec sheet tends to hide — in the earcup foam that flattens too quickly, the headband that starts to pinch, the ANC that wavers when the environment gets complex.

A $50 headphone with 72 hours of battery and 42dB ANC is either a remarkable value or a reminder that some numbers are easier to claim than to deliver consistently. The next few months of owner reviews will tell which one it is.
