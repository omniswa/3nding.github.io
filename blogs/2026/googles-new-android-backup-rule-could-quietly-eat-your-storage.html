Google's New Android Backup Rule Could Quietly Eat Your Storage

July 20, 2026 7-Minute Read

Your Android backups have never really been free. You just didn’t notice the cost.

Starting July 7, 2026, Google changed what counts against your Google Account storage. Every Android backup, not just Google Photos and MMS, now eats into the same 15GB free pool shared by Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If you’re an existing user, you’ll get 45 days’ notice by email before the change actually applies to your account. That’s the direct answer. Everything below is the part that actually matters: how much space this really takes, and whether you need to do anything about it.

What Actually Changed (And What Didn’t)

For years, Android backups worked on a kind of honor system. Photos and videos counted toward your storage because they’re big and Google needed a reason to sell you more space. But SMS messages, call history, device settings, and app settings sat outside the quota entirely. They just... existed, quietly, without touching your bill.

That era is over. Android backups are changing in two ways starting from July 7 — one you’ll like, one you probably won’t.

The one you won’t like: everything in your backup now counts toward your Google account storage. Not just photos anymore. Everything.

The one you will like: you now get individual on/off toggles for SMS and MMS messages, call history, and device settings, so you can decide what’s actually worth backing up. That’s a genuinely useful trade, even if it doesn’t feel like one at first.

Why Google Is Doing This Now

Google didn’t explain its reasoning in the notification email, and it probably never will. Companies rarely announce “we want more of your money” in plain language. But the timing tells its own story.

This backup change is part of a wider shift in Google’s storage policies. Back in May 2026, Google began testing a lower free storage limit for some new accounts, dropping from 15GB to just 5GB unless the account was linked to a phone number. Two separate changes, same underlying direction: shrink the free tier, or at least make it work harder for you.

That’s not a coincidence. It’s a pattern. Free cloud storage has been getting squeezed across the industry for years, and Google is simply moving Android in line with how Drive and Photos already behave.

How Much Storage This Actually Adds (Probably Less Than You Fear)

Here’s the reassuring part. For most people, this change is small enough to ignore.

Google’s own estimate puts the average increase at around 40MB per account. That’s the size of a couple of songs. It won’t push anyone over a 15GB limit on its own.

But averages hide outliers, and outliers are where this gets interesting. One journalist who dug into his own account found his actual increase was roughly 89.5MB, noticeably higher than the 80MB Google’s email had estimated for him, and well above the industry-wide 40MB average. That’s still tiny in absolute terms. But it shows the estimate is just that — an estimate, not a guarantee.

If you have thousands of text messages going back a decade, a call log that never gets cleared, or dozens of apps with their own backed-up settings, your number could run higher than the average. Not catastrophically higher. Just higher.

The Email You’ll Get (And What It Actually Says)

Google isn’t springing this on people without warning, which is worth giving credit for. Google has started emailing Android users with the subject line “New Android Backup Storage Policy & Controls.”

The email isn’t vague corporate filler. It explains that once the change takes effect, all data included in Android device backups will count toward your Google Account storage limit. It also tells you your personal number. One recipient’s email estimated his backup would increase by just 0.03GB, around 30MB, after the policy kicked in — proof that for a lot of people, this really is a non-event.

For existing users, the change takes effect 45 days after you receive the email, not 45 days from July 7. So two people could see this land on completely different dates depending on when Google gets around to notifying them. New accounts don’t get a grace period. They’re already living under the new rules.

What Happens If You Actually Run Out of Space

This is the part worth reading even if you’re confident your storage is fine, because “fine” can change.

If your account tips over its storage limit after this change applies, Google has been direct about the consequence: automatic Android backups will pause until you either free up storage or upgrade your Google storage plan. Not deleted. Not lost. Just paused, sitting in limbo until you fix the space problem.

But backups aren’t the only casualty of a full account. Gmail can stop sending and receiving mail, Drive stops accepting new files, and Photos stops backing up new images. None of that is new behavior caused by this policy change — it’s simply how Google storage limits have always worked. The backup change just adds one more thing competing for the same finite space.

If you’re already close to your limit, that’s the real risk here. Not the 40MB itself, but the 40MB landing on an account that was already one photo dump away from trouble.

The New Controls Are Genuinely Worth Using

Buried inside a change that sounds like a downgrade is a feature upgrade that’s easy to miss.

If you’re running Android 9 or later, you can now choose exactly which types of data and which individual apps are included in your backups. That means excluding the stuff you never actually want restored on a new phone anyway.

Finding the setting takes a slightly odd path. It’s not buried in the phone’s Settings app — it lives inside the Google One app instead, under a screen split into two sections: device data and app data, each with its own toggles.

Practically, here’s what to consider turning off:

  • Call history — useful for maybe two people who need a record for work, meaningless for most.
  • SMS and MMS — if you already use a separate messaging app for anything important, your native SMS archive may just be years of two-factor codes and delivery notifications.
  • Individual app settings — some apps back up huge caches you’ll never notice are missing.

None of this needs to happen today. But five minutes reviewing the list, once, saves you from wondering later why your storage crept up for no obvious reason.

What To Actually Do About It This Week

You don’t need to panic, and you don’t need to buy Google One immediately. But three small actions cover almost every scenario this change creates.

First, check your current storage. Open the Google One app, and look for your usage breakdown. If you’re already above 80% used, this is the moment to care.

Second, wait for or search for the email. It’ll tell you your specific estimated increase, which is more useful than any industry average.

Third, if the estimate looks meaningfully larger than 40MB, or you’re already tight on space, go into the new backup toggles and turn off whatever you don’t need restored on a future phone. Call history and old SMS threads are the easiest things to cut with zero downside.

For the overwhelming majority of Android users, this change will be invisible. A rounding error next to the photos already eating most of your quota. But now you know why your number moved, and you know exactly where to look if it moves more than expected.
