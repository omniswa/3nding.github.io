Spotify Adopts Apple's HLS for Video Podcasts

May 15, 2026 7-Minute Read

For most of its history, Spotify and Apple Podcasts have operated as closed ecosystems that tolerated each other’s existence without really cooperating. Creators had to make a choice: host on Spotify’s tools to get video monetization, or stick with a third-party host and distribute to Apple’s massive iOS audience—but not both, not cleanly. That forced tradeoff is now quietly coming apart. On May 14, 2026, Spotify announced it will adopt Apple’s HTTP Live Streaming (HLS) video technology across Spotify for Creators and its enterprise hosting platform, Megaphone. The practical effect is significant: creators will be able to distribute video podcasts to both Spotify and Apple Podcasts from a single setup, without restructuring anything.

What HLS Actually Does

HLS isn’t new—Apple developed it years ago to solve a specific problem with video on mobile devices. Instead of delivering a video file as one continuous stream, HLS breaks content into small segments and adapts quality in real time based on available bandwidth. A listener on fast home Wi-Fi gets a sharp, high-resolution feed. The same listener switching to a patchy cellular connection gets a seamless downgrade instead of a spinning buffer wheel.

Apple deployed HLS to Apple Podcasts in February 2026 and shipped the upgraded experience to iPhone, iPad, Vision Pro, and the web by the end of March. The upgrade also unlocked a few capabilities that matter more to creators than they might first appear: seamless switching between video and audio-only streams, offline downloads, and dynamic ad insertion.

That last one is the real commercial engine here. Dynamic ad insertion (DAI) lets platforms swap ads in and out of content after publication—serving different ads to different listeners based on geography, time, or targeting criteria. For video, that’s been harder to implement without a standardized delivery layer. HLS provides that layer.

The Creator Problem This Actually Solves

Before this, video podcast distribution on Spotify worked in a fragmented way. If you hosted your show on Spotify for Creators or Megaphone, you could upload video and access the Spotify Partner Program—a dual revenue model that pays creators from both Premium subscriber engagement and free-tier advertising. If you hosted on Libsyn, Audioboom, or any other third-party platform, you distributed to Spotify via RSS, which meant audio only. Video monetization required moving your hosting entirely.

That’s a real friction point for established shows with existing infrastructure, audience analytics, and ad deals built around their current host.

Spotify has been chipping away at this problem from two angles simultaneously. The Distribution API—announced in January 2026 and now live on Libsyn, Podigee, Audioboom, Audiomeans, and Podspace—lets creators on those platforms push video directly to Spotify without switching hosts. The HLS integration works in the opposite direction: creators already on Spotify’s tools can now distribute their video to Apple Podcasts without changing anything.

Together, they’re attempting to make Spotify the revenue infrastructure for video podcasters regardless of where those creators actually live.

The Numbers Behind the Push

The scale of Spotify’s video podcast investment is worth understanding. As of November 2025, more than 390 million users had streamed a video podcast on Spotify, across a catalog that now exceeds 530,000 shows. Video consumption on the platform grew more than 90% in the period following the Partner Program launch in January 2025. Creator payouts grew 300% in January 2025 year-over-year.

In January 2026, Spotify cut Partner Program eligibility thresholds by 80%—the required listener count dropped from 2,000 to 1,000, hours consumed from 10,000 to 2,000, and published episodes from 12 to just 3. That’s a deliberate move to bring smaller shows into the monetized ecosystem before competitors can.

Apple, for its part, came to video podcasting later but with significant distribution leverage. The iPhone’s install base and the default presence of Apple Podcasts on every iOS device means Apple’s platform reaches audiences that simply don’t use Spotify.

Why This Arrangement Makes Sense for Both Companies

The obvious question is why Apple would want Spotify—a direct competitor in the audio streaming space—benefiting from its technology.

The answer is that Apple’s interest in podcasting isn’t primarily about platform lock-in; it’s about the health of the format. Apple Podcasts has never had a strong monetization story for creators, and HLS on its own doesn’t change that. What Apple gains from Spotify’s adoption is broader HLS standardization, which makes Apple Podcasts more attractive to video creators who previously had little reason to invest in the platform. More video podcasts on Apple Podcasts means more engagement on the platform and more reason for creators to care about Apple’s audience.

Spotify, meanwhile, gains distribution reach it can’t build itself. Apple Podcasts listeners who never open Spotify can now watch a Spotify-hosted show. The creator stays in Spotify’s ecosystem, pays through Spotify’s tools, and earns through Spotify’s Partner Program—but their content appears on Apple’s platform. That’s a customer acquisition strategy dressed up as a technical integration.

The Open Web Concern Worth Noting

HLS is proprietary to Apple. It’s not the same as RSS, which has been the backbone of podcast distribution for two decades precisely because it belongs to no one.

The podcast industry’s historical strength—portability, independence, the ability to publish once and appear everywhere—has always rested on open standards. When two of the largest platforms in audio align around a proprietary video delivery protocol, there’s a structural concern: smaller hosting platforms, independent directories, and open podcast apps could find themselves on the outside of a technical standard they didn’t build and don’t control.

This isn’t an immediate crisis. RSS still governs audio distribution and shows no signs of disappearing. But video is where growth is happening, and if HLS becomes the default for video podcasting the same way it already dominates live streaming, the long-term architecture of podcast distribution shifts in a direction that concentrates power.

Dataconomy flagged this directly: HLS adoption could “result in a concentration of power within the podcasting industry.” It’s worth watching how smaller open-source podcast platforms respond over the next year.

What Creators Should Do With This Information

If you’re hosting on Spotify for Creators or Megaphone, the near-term action is simple: wait for rollout. Spotify says it’s “actively working on this integration in coordination with Apple” and will share timeline details soon. When it ships, distributing to Apple Podcasts should require no workflow changes on your end.

If you’re on one of the five platforms now connected to the Spotify Distribution API—Libsyn, Podigee, Audioboom, Audiomeans, or Podspace—video distribution to Spotify is already live. That includes access to the Partner Program’s monetization and video-specific analytics. You don’t need to touch your hosting setup.

If you’re on a platform not yet integrated, the practical advice is to wait and see which hosts move first. The API exists; it’s a matter of your platform activating it. Pressuring your host to prioritize the integration is a more productive move than platform-switching in anticipation.

The Broader Shift

What’s actually happening here is less a technical story than a structural one. For years, the major podcast platforms competed by building walls: exclusive content, proprietary formats, platform-specific monetization. That strategy has mostly failed to deliver the lock-in companies hoped for. Listeners follow shows, not platforms.

The new competitive logic appears to be about infrastructure. Spotify isn’t trying to win by keeping creators exclusive—it’s trying to win by being the most useful layer between creators and audiences, regardless of where those audiences listen. HLS adoption and the Distribution API are both moves in that direction. The creator stays on Spotify’s tools. The listener can be anywhere.

Whether that’s a durable advantage depends on execution. But for the first time in a while, Spotify and Apple are building in the same direction—and podcast creators are the direct beneficiaries.
