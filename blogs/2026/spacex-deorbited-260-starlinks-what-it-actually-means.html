SpaceX Deorbited 260 Starlinks: What It Actually Means

July 6, 2026 7-Minute Read

Most people read “SpaceX destroyed 260 satellites” and picture a malfunction. That reaction is backwards. According to SpaceX’s own filing with the Federal Communications Commission, the company intentionally deorbited 260 Starlink satellites between December 2025 and May 2026, and this is routine fleet maintenance, not a crisis. It’s the plan working exactly as designed.

The Filing, In Plain Numbers

SpaceX submits a semi-annual report to the FCC as a condition of its operating license. The latest one, filed around July 1, 2026, discloses that 260 satellites came down through controlled atmospheric re-entry over the prior six months. Of those, 176 belonged to the original first-generation Starlink constellation, while the remaining spacecraft were newer second-generation models. Another 349 satellites have already been pulled from active service and are simply waiting their turn to be pushed down.

That’s the whole story in one paragraph. Everything else is context.

Why “Deorbited” Doesn’t Mean “Broken”

Here’s the part that trips people up. A satellite doesn’t need to fail for SpaceX to bring it down. Starlink satellites are intentionally designed with an operational lifespan of approximately five years. When a satellite hits that age, or starts showing early signs of battery or telemetry degradation, SpaceX retires it on purpose, before it ever loses the ability to steer itself.

This matters because a dead satellite that still has fuel is a very different problem from a dead satellite with no fuel left. A satellite with no control authority just sits there, an unguided piece of metal in one of the busiest patches of sky humans have ever created. SpaceX’s whole strategy is built around never letting that happen. Retire early. Steer while you still can. Burn up on command instead of drifting for years.

The Reliability Number the FCC Actually Cares About

Regulators don’t grade this on vibes. The FCC requires megaconstellation operators to hit a post-mission disposal reliability rate of at least 95 percent, meaning at least 95 out of every 100 dead satellites must be actively steered down rather than left to decay on their own over decades. SpaceX’s filing claims a rate exceeding 99%, with the vast majority of its retired fleet descending safely within six months of taking its deorbit commands.

That four-point gap between “required” and “achieved” sounds small. It isn’t. At a constellation size north of 10,000 satellites, the difference between 95 percent and 99 percent is hundreds of dead spacecraft that either get cleaned up promptly or linger as long-term collision risks. Small percentages get loud fast at this scale.

The Pace Is Speeding Up, and That’s Mostly a Good Sign

Six-month deorbit totals aren’t flat. They’re climbing, then dipping, then climbing again, tracking the age of successive satellite generations. The prior six-month window saw 218 satellites come down, so 260 represents an increase. Go back one more cycle and the number jumps to 472 between December 2024 and May 2025, still the highest six-month total on record. Astronomer Jonathan McDowell, who tracks the constellation independently, has said SpaceX mostly finished retiring the oldest satellites, though re-entry rates remain higher than back in 2023.

Read that pattern correctly and it tells a fairly boring story. SpaceX launched a huge wave of first-generation satellites years ago. Those satellites are now aging out in bunches, not all at once. The bumps in the data are generational, like a large company’s IT department replacing a batch of laptops purchased the same fiscal year. Nothing about the shape of that curve looks like failure.

What Happens to the Hardware Physically

The disposal method itself is almost anticlimactic. Each retiring satellite uses its remaining propellant to gradually lower its orbit before entering Earth’s atmosphere, where aerodynamic heating destroys the satellite completely, eliminating the risk of large debris reaching the surface. Nothing survives the trip. No panels crash into a field somewhere. It’s a controlled burn, engineered to leave nothing behind for someone to trip over.

That’s the point, actually. Recovering a dead satellite in orbit and bringing it home intact would cost far more than the satellite is worth. Complete incineration solves the ground-safety problem cheaply, at scale, thousands of times over.

The Weight Problem Nobody Talks About Enough

Here’s where the story gets more interesting than a simple headline count. Not all deorbiting satellites are the same size, and that difference matters more than the raw satellite count does. First-generation units weighed roughly 260 to 295 kilograms, while second-generation spacecraft range from approximately 800 to 1,250 kilograms. A constellation shifting toward heavier, more capable satellites means every future re-entry vaporizes roughly three to four times the mass of an older one.

So the “260 satellites” figure understates where this is heading. Fewer, bigger satellites still means more total material burning into the upper atmosphere over time, since SpaceX is rapidly backfilling the lower orbital planes with larger, high-capacity Starlink v2 Mini and full-scale Block 3 satellites as older units clear out.

The Question Science Hasn’t Closed the Book On

This is the genuinely unresolved part, and it deserves more attention than a passing mention. Repeated atmospheric burn-up of satellite hardware releases aluminum oxide and other combustion byproducts into the stratosphere. Some scientists warn that a continuous influx of alumina from thousands of deorbiting satellites could alter the stratosphere’s albedo, potentially affecting global solar radiation absorption and inducing localized ozone layer depletion. Nobody has definitively proven large-scale harm yet. Nobody has ruled it out either. That’s the honest state of the science, and pretending otherwise in either direction isn’t accurate.

Regulators are wrestling with the same uncertainty from a different angle. Satellite operations have traditionally sidestepped environmental review through a NEPA categorical exclusion, and the FCC is now weighing whether to make that exemption formal and permanent by classifying orbital activity as occurring outside U.S. territorial jurisdiction. Environmental groups want the opposite outcome: they’re pushing to eliminate that exclusion entirely so operators would need full environmental impact statements before getting new launch licenses. Two agencies, two instincts, one open question that six-month filings like this one keep forcing back into view.

The Orbit-Lowering Move Happening at the Same Time

There’s a second, related story unfolding in parallel that context this well. Separate from the deorbit report, SpaceX is lowering roughly 4,400 of its Starlink satellites from their current orbit down to about 480 kilometers over the course of 2026. The reasoning ties directly back to disposal speed. Lower orbits mean thinner atmosphere clearing debris faster, so if a satellite does fail on orbit, it deorbits more quickly, with lowering translating to over an 80 percent reduction in ballistic decay time during solar minimum, turning four-plus years into a matter of months.

Put the two data points together and a clear pattern emerges. SpaceX isn’t just retiring old hardware faster. It’s actively re-engineering the entire constellation’s geometry so that anything that does fail, planned or not, falls out of the sky faster than it used to. That’s a defensible safety posture. It’s also, not coincidentally, a strategy that keeps regulators comfortable with a constellation that keeps growing past 10,000 spacecraft.

What This Means If You’re Watching Starlink Closely

None of this points to Starlink hitting a wall. If anything, the numbers describe a maturing industrial operation running its replacement cycle on schedule, at a scale no competing satellite broadband project currently matches. The things actually worth tracking going forward are narrower and more specific than “is Starlink failing.” Watch whether the FCC’s proposed NEPA exemption for satellite operations goes through, since that decision will shape how much environmental disclosure every future constellation operator has to produce. Watch whether the six-month deorbit totals keep climbing as second-generation satellites age into their own five-year retirement window, since that’s where the real mass-in-the-atmosphere question starts to bite. And watch whether independent researchers turn preliminary alumina and ozone concerns into anything resembling a firm conclusion, because right now that debate is still open, not settled.

The 260 number itself isn’t the headline. It’s a data point in a much longer, still-unfolding story about what it actually costs, physically and atmospherically, to keep a 10,000-plus satellite network running forever.
