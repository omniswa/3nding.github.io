Venmo Finally Makes Privacy the Default

May 12, 2026 7-Minute Read

For eight years, Venmo operated on a simple, unspoken assumption: your financial activity is a social event. Every payment you sent—to your landlord, your therapist, your dealer for “🌿🌿”—was broadcast by default to anyone who happened to look. The company wasn’t shy about it. When pressure mounted in 2024, PayPal reportedly described the public-by-default design as a feature, not a bug. Then, in May 2026, they quietly changed course.

Venmo’s privacy redesign flips the default: new users’ transaction posts will now be visible to friends only, not the entire public. It’s a small toggle with large implications for a platform used by roughly 90 million Americans.

Eight Years Is a Long Time to Ignore a Warning

The privacy problem wasn’t subtle or hypothetical. In 2018, a researcher named Do Thi Duc analyzed more than 200 million Venmo transactions pulled straight from the app’s open API. The stories she reconstructed included a cannabis dealer’s transaction history and a couple’s slow-motion public breakup—complete with notes like “Please leave me alone.” [9to5Mac] The data wasn’t hacked. It was just... there.

By 2018, Venmo settled a case with the FTC over misleading claims about “bank-grade security,” after which it removed the global feed and added some privacy controls for friends lists. [Onerep] But the core problem—public by default—remained intact.

Then in 2021, BuzzFeed News found Joe Biden’s Venmo account and, through his public friend list, mapped out his inner circle. Venmo added an option to hide your friend list after that incident [Engadget] , but again, the philosophical default didn’t shift.

The issue returned in 2024 when a public Venmo profile revealed JD Vance’s network of wealthy financiers, tech executives, and Yale Law School alumni—a contradiction to his populist political brand. [9to5Mac] At that point, PayPal claimed this was a feature, not a bug, and declined to fix it. [9to5Mac]

Whatever changed internally between then and now, the result is the same: Venmo blinked.

What the Redesign Actually Changes

The update is more substantive than a single settings toggle, though that’s the headline.

New users’ posts will only be visible to their friends by default, rather than the general public. Those who want full public visibility or full privacy—”just me”—will need to actively choose that during onboarding or in settings afterward. [Cybernews]

That distinction matters more than it sounds. Default settings aren’t just suggestions—they’re where behavior is actually determined. Most people never open the settings menu. They accept whatever path the app presents. By changing the default from public to friends-only, Venmo is effectively changing the behavior of every new user who never thinks about privacy at all.

The redesign also includes an updated payment screen that shows the transaction’s privacy status before it’s sent—letting the sender confirm whether the payment will be private, visible only to friends, or publicly visible. [Cybernews] That pre-send confirmation is genuinely useful. It converts a setting most people forget about into something visible at the moment it matters.

The rollout is staged, starting with a new look for the feed on iOS and Android over the coming weeks. [Cybernews]

The Business Logic Behind the Privacy Shift

It would be easy to read this as a pure ethics win, but the incentive structure is worth understanding. Venmo is attempting to transition from a niche peer-to-peer app into a comprehensive “money movement app for the next generation,” including merchant services and community commerce features. [Time News]

A new “Give a Shoutout” feature lets users promote local businesses directly in the feed. There are reaction buttons and quick-action shortcuts like “Pay Again” and “Say Thanks.” The social layer is getting more refined, not removed.

This is the tension: Venmo is leaning harder into social features while simultaneously claiming to take privacy more seriously. The two aren’t necessarily contradictory, but they do require careful calibration. A tighter default audience for transactions actually makes the social layer more usable—people are more willing to engage when they control who’s watching.

Venmo SVP and GM Alexis Sowa framed the change as being about giving users “more visibility and control over what they share” because user trust is “critical” to the Venmo experience. [GSMArena] That’s corporate language, but the underlying logic is sound: a payment app that feels like surveillance eventually loses users.

The Unanswered Question About Contacts

There’s a gap worth noting. It’s still not clear whether contacts—your friends list—will be private by default under the new redesign. [9to5Mac] That’s exactly the vulnerability that exposed Biden’s inner circle in 2021 and Vance’s network in 2024. Transaction posts going friends-only is meaningful, but if your contact graph remains publicly searchable, the social mapping problem persists.

Groups like the Electronic Frontier Foundation and Mozilla had previously urged Venmo to change its default settings, arguing that public transactions can reveal sensitive personal data. [Cybernews] Their concern extended beyond individual payments to the aggregate picture that emerges when you can see who pays whom, how often, and with what notes. Friends lists are a significant part of that picture.

For now, if you’re an existing user, none of the new defaults apply to you automatically. Your current settings—whatever they are—stay in place unless you change them manually.

What Existing Users Should Do Right Now

The redesign targets new signups. If you’ve had a Venmo account for years, your transaction history may already be public, and the new defaults won’t retroactively change that.

Here’s what to check:

  • Default privacy setting: Go to Settings → Privacy → Default Privacy Setting. Set it to Private unless you have a specific reason not to.
  • Past transactions: You can bulk-change existing transaction visibility from the same menu. It takes about 30 seconds.
  • Friends list: In Settings → Privacy, there’s a separate toggle for who can see your friends list. Unless you’ve already changed it, it may still be public.
  • Transaction notes: Even on private transactions, notes like “rent 🏠” or “therapy 💊” are visible to both parties. Write them accordingly.

The last point is one most guides skip. Dollar amounts tell one story. Notes often tell a more specific one. In the security world, this is called contextual exposure—individual data points that seem benign until they’re aggregated. Venmo notes are particularly rich because people write them casually, the way they’d text a friend.

A Better Late Than Never Reckoning

The cynical read is that Venmo moved only because the reputational cost of staying put finally exceeded the engagement value of public feeds. That’s probably accurate. Companies rarely make privacy-protective changes for purely principled reasons—especially when the privacy failure was described internally as a feature.

But the practical result is the same either way. A payment app used by 90 million people will now default to a setting that doesn’t passively expose user behavior to strangers, journalists, researchers, or anyone with a browser and ten minutes to spare.

For new users, that protection is automatic. For everyone else, it requires a few deliberate taps.

The better question—one that will take months to answer—is whether the change represents a genuine design philosophy or just a PR pivot ahead of a product refresh. The friends-list privacy gap, the staged rollout, and the fact that this took eight years after the original warning all suggest Venmo is still moving slower than the problem demands.

That said: the default changed. On a platform where defaults are the policy, that’s not nothing.
