Apple's New Siri AI: Worth the Two-Year Wait?

June 9, 2026 8-Minute Read

Siri has been a punchline for two years. That context matters enormously when you try to evaluate what Apple announced at WWDC on June 8, 2026, without getting swept up in the relief that something—anything—finally arrived.

Apple officially unveiled a new AI-powered version of Siri at WWDC 2026, nearly two years after first introducing Apple Intelligence and promising a smarter, more personalized assistant. [Dexerto] The product has a new name—Siri AI—and, crucially, a new architecture. Whether it closes the gap with ChatGPT and Gemini is a question that won’t be answered until it ships.

Siri Wasn’t Just Late—It Was Sued Into Existence

The delay wasn’t a quiet engineering struggle. It was public, expensive, and embarrassing in the specific way that only Apple embarrassments can be.

Apple settled a $250 million consumer class action last month over marketing Siri AI features in 2024 that were not ready when the iPhone 16 launched. The personalized Siri capabilities originally advertised at WWDC 2024 were delayed indefinitely in March 2025. [TNW | Apple] Apple also pulled TV advertisements it had used to promote the unreleased version of Siri. One executive reportedly described the timing as particularly “ugly.”

Apple reshuffled its AI executive ranks in 2025, with AI head John Giannandrea reportedly replaced by Mike Rockwell—who had headed the Vision Pro team—and former Google and Microsoft AI researcher Amar Subramanya announced as Apple’s new vice president of AI. [Information Age] Leadership changes mid-project are rarely a sign of confidence. They are a sign of a company that knew its current trajectory wasn’t working.

That organizational reset, combined with an outside partnership, is the real story behind Monday’s announcement.

What “Built From the Ground Up” Actually Means

Apple used the phrase “rebuilt from the ground up” during the keynote. That phrasing gets used a lot and usually means little. Here, it means something specific.

Apple Intelligence’s new architecture is based on “next-generation Apple Foundation Models” made in collaboration with Google Gemini. The architecture allows for deep, contextual AI experience: it can understand images, broad world knowledge, what data is on your device, what’s on your screen, and more. [The Shortcut]

The Google connection is not Siri routing your questions to Gemini, the way it previously handed off to ChatGPT. Apple is continuing to position Siri AI as a deeply integrated and privacy-focused alternative to standalone AI chatbots [Tbreak Media] —Google’s models are woven into Apple’s own Foundation Models infrastructure, not bolted on top.

Why does that distinction matter? Because integration at the model layer means Siri AI can theoretically hold context across apps, your calendar, your messages, and your photos in a way that a third-party handoff cannot. The assistant knows where it is in your life. That’s the design goal, at least.

The Three Things Siri AI Can Now Do That the Old Siri Couldn’t

On-screen context is the first real behavioral change. The assistant can understand what’s currently displayed on a user’s screen and take action based on that context. [Dexerto] This sounds incremental. It isn’t. The old Siri operated in a narrow lane—it could set a timer or play a song, but it had no idea what you were looking at. This version can see your screen, interpret it, and act on it. The mechanism is Apple’s Visual Intelligence layer, now baked into Siri AI directly.

Cross-app task execution is the second. Siri AI can answer factual questions using information from the web or a user’s personal data while also carrying out tasks such as drafting emails, checking calendars, and interacting with apps. [Dexerto] The word “interacting with apps” is doing a lot of work there. It signals that Siri AI isn’t just reading your data—it’s operating inside other apps on your behalf. That’s a meaningful architectural shift.

Conversational follow-up is the third. It remembers what you said thirty seconds ago and builds on it. The old Siri forgot your question the moment it answered it. A model that maintains conversation state across a session is a fundamentally more useful tool, because real tasks rarely fit inside a single command.

The Privacy Architecture—And Why Apple Is Betting Its Differentiation on It

The new Apple Foundation Models run both on-device and on servers through Private Cloud Compute, with Apple maintaining that personal data is not stored or accessible to anyone—including Apple—when cloud processing is used. [Digit]

This is a three-tier stack: simple tasks run entirely on your device (fast, zero data exposure), complex tasks route to Private Cloud Compute (Apple-controlled servers, cryptographically sealed), and general knowledge queries can draw on broader models. The new Siri AI will be able to access and interact with all the apps and data on a user’s iPhone, but with strong privacy controls, according to Craig Federighi, Apple’s senior vice president of software engineering. [Variety]

Apple’s competitors process your data on centralized servers and retain it for model training. That’s the trade-off that has always funded “free” AI. Apple is betting a meaningful slice of iPhone buyers will pay a premium—in hardware and iCloud subscriptions—to keep that data local. Whether that bet holds as AI becomes commoditized is the central question of Apple’s next five years.

What Siri AI Looks Like in Practice

The new Siri, dubbed Siri AI, will be accessible through a standalone app, and also through the search function on the home screen of Apple devices and in various other apps, like Photos. [NPR]

The standalone app is a genuine signal. Apple doesn’t create new first-party apps casually—the last major one was a while back. A dedicated app means Siri AI is being positioned as a destination you go to, not just a reflex you invoke with a button. That’s a direct play for the habitual use that ChatGPT has built.

Users will be able to adjust the pace and expressivity of Siri AI’s conversational experience [Variety] —more natural synthetic voices, tunable delivery. This is smaller but not trivial. Voice interface fatigue is real. If the assistant sounds less robotic, people use it more.

The Catch: Hardware Requirements, EU Lockout, and a Beta Label

Siri AI will launch as a beta in September 2026 in English, with more languages following. It will not be available on iPhone or iPad in the EU due to Apple’s dispute with regulators over the Digital Markets Act, and will not launch in China initially due to regulatory requirements. [Digit]

That EU absence is not a footnote. Europe represents a substantial portion of Apple’s premium user base. Apple argues that Digital Markets Act interoperability requirements conflict with the privacy architecture it designed for Siri AI. iOS 27 Extensions will let users set a third-party AI model, such as Claude or ChatGPT, as their default assistant—a concession to both competitive pressure and the EU’s Digital Markets Act requirements that previously forced Apple to pause its AI rollout in Europe. [TNW | Apple]

The advanced features of Siri in iOS 27 are expected to be available only on newer devices, such as the iPhone 15 Pro or later models, due to hardware requirements. [Geeky Gadgets] That limits the install base at launch. The on-device processing architecture Apple built requires the Neural Engine performance in its A17 Pro chip and later. Older hardware simply can’t run the local model layer.

September beta. English only. No EU. No China. Limited hardware. These aren’t fatal flaws—every major software rollout stages this way—but they’re worth knowing before calibrating expectations.

What the Stock Market Said—And Why It’s Worth Noting

Apple’s share price fell close to 2% after the news, though analysts say the changes have potential. [NPR]

Markets priced in this announcement months ago. The drop is a classic “sell the news” reaction, but it also reflects something real: two years of anticipation had generated enormous expectations, and a September beta with a restricted rollout lands softer than “available today.” Investors want to see retention metrics from Siri AI users, not a developer conference demo.

That’s fair. Apple’s AI business case rests on whether Siri AI becomes a reason to buy new iPhone hardware, not just a feature users try once and forget. The old Siri never accomplished that.

What This Actually Changes—For You, Right Now

Not much. Today.

Developer betas are live. A public beta is expected in July. A full release is anticipated in September. [Geeky Gadgets] If you have an iPhone 15 Pro or newer, you’ll be able to try it then. If you’re outside the US, UK, Australia, Canada, and a handful of other English-speaking markets, you’ll wait longer.

The more consequential question is what Siri AI means for the 2.35 billion active Apple devices worldwide that will eventually run it. Apple confirmed that iOS 27 will support every iPhone model currently running iOS 26, including the iPhone 11 series and newer devices. [The Sunday Guardian] Most of those devices won’t run Siri AI’s most demanding features. But the infrastructure Apple is building now—the Foundation Models framework, the Private Cloud Compute architecture, the app-level integration—is designed to scale forward, not backward.

Apple has always played a long hardware-software game. Siri AI is, in that sense, less a finished product than a staked claim. It says: this is where the assistant is going, here is the privacy architecture that will carry it there, and here is the hardware cycle that will fund it.

Two years late. But the architecture, for the first time, looks like it was worth building carefully.
