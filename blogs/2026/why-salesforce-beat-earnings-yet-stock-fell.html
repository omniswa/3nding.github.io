Why Salesforce Beat Earnings Yet Stock Fell

May 28, 2026 8-Minute Read

On May 27, 2026, Salesforce reported first-quarter fiscal 2027 results that should have pleased investors. The company’s adjusted earnings per share hit $3.88, demolishing the consensus estimate of $3.12. Revenue came in at $11.13 billion, beating expectations of $11.05 billion. Yet the stock barely moved in after-hours trading.

By the next morning, the real story emerged. Salesforce guided for second-quarter revenue between $11.27 billion and $11.35 billion, slightly below the $11.36 billion consensus forecast. The company’s stock was down almost 33% for 2026 overall, despite crushing quarterly numbers. This wasn’t a failure of execution. It was a failure of reassurance.

The gap between earnings and stock movement reveals something fundamental about how software companies are valued in 2026. Traditional metrics no longer matter. What investors want is proof of survival in an AI-transformed market.

The Earnings Did Look Good

Numbers don’t lie. Salesforce’s revenue increased 13% year over year during the quarter, which ended on April 30. Subscription and support revenue from the Agentforce product line reached $6.91 billion, up almost 9% year-over-year. Agentforce annual recurring revenue hit $800 million, up 169% year-over-year, and total Agentforce and Data 360 ARR reached $2.9 billion, an increase of more than 200% year-over-year.

This is not a company in decline. A 13% revenue beat in a mature enterprise software market is respectable. The EPS beat of 24% reflects disciplined cost management and strong operational leverage. From a traditional financial analyst’s perspective, this is exactly the kind of quarter that drives stock appreciation.

But traditional financial analysis stopped mattering around mid-2025. What replaced it was something harder to model and more existential: competitive disruption fear.

Where the Guidance Fell Short

The numbers that actually moved the market were smaller and more ominous. Salesforce guided for Q2 revenue between $11.27 billion and $11.35 billion, below the $11.36 billion LSEG consensus. The miss itself—a fraction of a percentage point—was trivial on paper. In context, it meant something else entirely: Salesforce could not credibly project acceleration.

Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff called agentic AI the “biggest growth opportunity” for the company and its clients, while CFO Robin Washington said the company was sticking with guidance for “organic revenue acceleration” in the second half of fiscal 2027. The framing was right. The numbers were not.

When a software company says its biggest growth opportunity lies ahead but cannot show it in near-term guidance, investors hear: management is not confident. That distinction—between saying and showing—determines valuations in 2026.

The Real Problem: The SaaSpocalypse Question

None of this is about Salesforce alone. The sector-wide selloff has been referred to as a “SaaSpocalypse,” driven by concerns that AI agents and advanced coding tools from companies like OpenAI and Anthropic may reduce dependence on legacy SaaS applications. It’s the question every enterprise software CEO now faces, usually not said aloud but always implied: if AI can automate what we do, why pay premium subscription fees?

Investors increasingly want proof that AI products can generate meaningful new revenue fast enough to offset slowing growth, pricing pressure, and the broader “SaaSpocalypse” fears. This is not skepticism about Salesforce specifically. It’s skepticism about whether any legacy software company can grow its way out of disruption before disruption scales.

Agentforce is growing, but at a scale that matters to a $100+ billion company, it’s still noise. In the 15 months since Agentforce launched, Salesforce closed over 29,000 Agentforce deals. That sounds impressive in isolation. Relative to Salesforce’s existing base of hundreds of thousands of customers, and relative to the subscription revenue being challenged by AI automation, it signals a company betting heavily on a new product that is not yet big enough to move the needle on growth rates.

The market’s logic is harsh but mathematically sound: if Salesforce can’t show meaningful acceleration despite launching its largest AI initiative in years, perhaps there is no acceleration to show. Perhaps the legacy business is being hollowed out from below while the new business remains too small to fill the gap.

Why Guidance Matters More Than Earnings Now

This represents a real shift in how the market values software companies. A decade ago, beating quarterly earnings was the path to stock appreciation. Misses were punished; beats were rewarded, usually with multi-percent gains. The relationship was direct and linear.

In 2026, the relationship inverted. Beating on a past quarter that’s already known is cheap credibility. What investors price in is forward guidance—the company’s own assertion about what it believes will happen. A company that beats the last three quarters but guides conservatively is signaling that future growth is harder than the market wants to believe.

Salesforce beat on past results while guiding for modest growth ahead. The market interpreted this as: you’ve extracted what you can from the legacy business, and the new business isn’t ready to compensate. This interpretation is logical whether you’re a hedge fund manager, a VC, or a long-term shareholder trying to understand where returns will come from.

The Informatica Wrinkle

One complication: Salesforce bought Informatica for $9.6 billion in November, with Informatica contributing $428 million in subscription revenue. Large acquisitions always inject optionality and complexity into guidance. Salesforce had to model Informatica’s growth trajectory, integration risks, and customer retention. Conservative guidance on an integration-year quarter is defensible.

Yet this also illustrates a deeper challenge. Salesforce spent billions acquiring its way into data infrastructure because the market demanded proof of AI capabilities. The capital deployment was reasonable, but it also signaled that organic innovation alone wasn’t sufficient to address the AI opportunity. When you’re betting your future on a $9.6 billion acquisition, margins tighten and execution risk rises.

What The Numbers Say About Enterprise Spending

Salesforce has been aggressively marketing its AI capabilities, particularly through its Agentforce platform for autonomous agents and its Einstein AI suite. The pitch to customers is compelling: let AI handle more of the repetitive CRM work so your sales team can focus on closing deals. Yet compelling pitches don’t always convert at the pace required to move enterprise valuations.

Enterprises are adopting AI carefully. The muted FY 2026 guidance raised concerns about the pace of AI-driven revenue growth, with investors watching for clearer signals of acceleration. Agentforce adoption grows, but revenue impact remains limited as enterprises take a measured AI approach. This is the gap between demand and deployment velocity. Everyone agrees AI is valuable. Almost everyone is moving slowly to integrate it, test it, and trust it.

Salesforce can’t force enterprise customers to move faster. If customers don’t see autonomous AI agents as a primary budget priority, Agentforce adoption remains good-to-strong rather than explosive. Guidance that reflects this reality—measured rather than accelerating—is honest. But honesty about slow adoption doesn’t move stock prices.

The Valuation Regime Has Changed

As of Wednesday’s close, Salesforce shares were down 33% so far in 2026, while the S&P 500 index had gained about 10%. That gap—33% down versus 10% up—represents a fundamental repricing of what Salesforce is worth. The old SaaS valuation model was predicated on predictable, secular growth. The new model is predicated on defensibility and AI adaptability.

Salesforce checks both boxes competently: it’s defensible (customers are locked in), and it’s adapted (Agentforce exists and is growing). What it hasn’t proven is that these adaptations are sufficient to restore the growth premium the stock once commanded. Until quarterly guidance shows unmistakable acceleration, that premium stays depressed.

This is why earnings beats matter less. An earnings beat that doesn’t resolve the underlying question—can you grow again?—is noise. It’s proof that you’re managing the current business well, which investors already knew. The stock only re-rates when guidance demonstrates future growth, not just current profitability.

The Competitive Context

Salesforce is not alone in facing this repricing. The pressure is forcing major software companies to rapidly reposition themselves as AI infrastructure providers rather than traditional application vendors. Workday, ServiceNow, Adobe—every large SaaS company is racing to show that its new AI products can grow fast enough to offset legacy application erosion. None have yet given the market unambiguous proof.

This creates a temporary dynamic where all large software stocks are suspect, regardless of earnings quality. The rerating is sector-wide, not company-specific. Salesforce’s 33% decline in 2026 is part of a broader pattern in which software companies with $30 billion+ market caps are trading at 2016-era valuations because investors no longer trust 2025-era growth rates.

What Happens Next

The story will resolve when one of two things occurs. Either enterprise AI spending accelerates visibly—meaning next quarter’s guidance looks materially better than this quarter’s—or the market accepts that software growth has reset to mid-single digits permanently, and prices accordingly. There is no scenario in which strong earnings alone drive a stock re-rate. There is only the scenario in which guidance suggests the company can grow again, not just execute well today.

Salesforce’s challenge is that it’s executing perfectly on the business it has while building the business it needs. Investors are paying attention only to the latter. Until that business proves it can drive material revenue growth, earnings beats remain footnotes in a longer, slower story.

The market is not punishing Salesforce for missing. It’s ignoring past success as irrelevant to the question that actually matters: can you prove you still belong in a portfolio of growth companies? Until the answer is obviously yes, no earnings beat will matter much.
