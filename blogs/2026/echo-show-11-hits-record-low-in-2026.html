Echo Show 11 Hits Record Low in 2026

June 18, 2026 5-Minute Read

The Echo Show line spent years being difficult to recommend. Too expensive to be an impulse buy, too limited to justify the price over a cheap tablet. The new Echo Show 11 changes that math—and right now, the price is making the decision even easier.

Amazon has dropped the Echo Show 11 to $150 as a Prime Early Deal, down from its regular $220—a 32% discount that represents the lowest price the device has hit since launch. [Gizmodo] That’s not a holiday blip or a clearance event. It’s Amazon signaling that it wants this hardware in as many homes as possible before Prime Day officially kicks off.

This Is a Hardware Play Dressed Up as a Sale

Amazon doesn’t discount aggressively because it’s feeling generous. It discounts because the device is a delivery mechanism for a service—and right now, that service is Alexa+.

Any customer who orders a new Echo device gets access to Alexa+ out of the box. [Amazon] The cheaper the hardware gets, the wider the Alexa+ install base grows. This is the same logic Amazon has used with Kindle for fifteen years: sell the device at cost, profit from the ecosystem. The Echo Show 11 at $150 is not a product on sale. It’s a subscription funnel with a great screen attached.

The Echo Show 11 launched in fall 2025 at $220, saw modest Black Friday deals, then Amazon began cutting deeper—first in February, again in mid-March, and now to $150 ahead of Prime Day 2026. [9to5Toys] The price trajectory is not accidental. It’s a deliberate drumbeat pulling the device into mainstream impulse-buy territory.

What Actually Changed in This Generation

Previous Echo Shows were fine. This one is different in ways that matter.

At the core of the Echo Show 11 is Amazon’s custom AZ3 Pro chip—the more advanced of two new silicon designs—which adds support for state-of-the-art language models and vision transformers on top of its upgraded audio processing capabilities. [Amazon] That matters because it means more AI computation happens on the device itself, reducing latency and making interactions feel snappier in a way that prior generations couldn’t deliver.

Gone are the chunky, plastic-heavy designs that made previous Echo Shows feel like budget tablets bolted to speakers. The new model sports a sleek, fabric-wrapped speaker base with a thin, floating display that wouldn’t look out of place in a modern kitchen. [Techbuzz] That’s not a minor aesthetic update. It’s the difference between a device that gets shoved in a drawer and one that stays on a counter.

The 11-inch display gives 60% more viewing area than the Echo Show 8, and the spatial audio system features a 2.8-inch woofer with crisper vocals, a wider soundstage, and up to twice the bass of the prior Echo Show 8. [Gizmodo] For kitchen use—recipes, video calls, background music—that combination is legitimately useful in a way the smaller displays never quite managed.

The Sensor Stack Is the Sleeper Feature

Most people will buy this for the screen. The more interesting story is the Omnisense platform underneath it.

Omnisense leverages a range of sensors simultaneously—the 13-megapixel camera, audio pickups, ultrasound, Wi-Fi radar, an accelerometer, and Wi-Fi CSI—to allow Alexa to intelligently act on events happening in and around your home. [Amazon] This is presence detection that doesn’t require you to say anything. Walk into the kitchen, the display lights up with your calendar. The device recognizes your face. It’s not a gimmick—it’s the kind of ambient computing Amazon has been working toward since it launched the original Echo in 2014.

Early Alexa+ users are already talking to Alexa over 2x more than before, having deeper conversations on any topic and relying on it to complete more tasks like controlling smart home devices, booking reservations, and managing family calendars. [Amazon] That engagement metric tells you something real: when the AI gets meaningfully better, the hardware usage goes up with it.

The Part Amazon Doesn’t Advertise

This is not a perfect device. Two friction points are worth knowing before you buy.

Amazon removed the physical camera shutter in favor of a software mute button—a privacy regression that users who previously relied on the hardware kill-switch will notice immediately. [Techbuzz] That physical shutter was one of the stronger trust signals on older Echo Shows. Replacing it with software controls is a step backward, even if the mic/camera disable button remains functional.

Amazon has also confirmed these devices will still display full-screen ads between content—a decision that prioritizes revenue over user experience on what is now a premium device. [Techbuzz] At $220, that was a harder trade-off to accept. At $150, it’s more tolerable. But it’s still there, and worth knowing.

Who Should Buy This Right Now

If you’re running an older Echo Show—particularly anything pre-2024—the AZ3 Pro chip and Omnisense sensors represent a genuine generational leap, not a marginal iteration. The upgrade makes sense.

If you’ve never owned a smart display and you’re curious, $150 is the lowest barrier the category has ever offered for an 11-inch device with this hardware spec. The smart home integration works with thousands of Alexa-compatible devices without requiring a separate hub—lights, thermostats, cameras, and video doorbells all connect natively. [Gizmodo] For someone building a connected home from scratch, that consolidation alone justifies the entry point.

If you care deeply about privacy hardware controls or want a full app ecosystem beyond Prime Video and Netflix, the calculus is more complicated. The device is capable. The trade-offs are real.

At $150, the Echo Show 11 lands at a price that makes the 11-inch upgrade a no-brainer for most people. [Gizmodo] The question was never whether the hardware was good enough. It was always whether the price made the argument for you. Right now, it does.
