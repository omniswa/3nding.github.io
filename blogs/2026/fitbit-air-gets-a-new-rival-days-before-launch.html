Fitbit Air Gets a New Rival Days Before Launch

May 22, 2026 9-Minute Read

Google confirmed the Fitbit Air on May 7, with pre-orders live and shipping set for May 26. Then, with barely a week to go, the Luna Band officially entered the conversation—pricing confirmed, waitlist open, and a feature set designed to make the Fitbit Air look like the cautious option. It’s not the only competitor circling. Garmin’s Cirqa is leaking faster than the company can plug the holes. What started as Google’s play to crack a niche Whoop dominated now looks like the beginning of a genuine category war.

Why Screenless Trackers Are Having a Moment

The timing here is not coincidental. Whoop has spent years building a devoted following among athletes willing to pay $199–$239 a year for recovery data. No screen, no distractions—just heart rate variability, sleep quality, and a daily readiness score. The concept always made sense. The subscription never sat well with casual users.

The Fitbit Air launching at $99 is pushing screenless fitness trackers into the mainstream in 2026, turning what was once an athlete-focused niche into a legitimate consumer category. Google’s brand reach does that. When Steph Curry casually wore an unreleased Fitbit band in his Instagram content for weeks before the official announcement, it reached an audience that had never heard of Whoop. That audience is now buying $99 trackers.

The underlying appeal is real: most people who own a smartwatch don’t need another screen on their wrist. They want to know how well they slept, whether their body is primed for a hard session, and whether they’re trending toward burnout. None of that requires a display. It requires accurate sensors and software that doesn’t bury the insight under four taps.

The Fitbit Air: What Google Actually Built

The Fitbit Air is a screenless wrist-worn tracker priced at $99 that records activities, vital health metrics, and sleep continuously—and unlike a Pixel Watch, it works on both Android and iOS. That cross-platform compatibility matters more than it might sound. Google’s wearable strategy has historically been Android-first to the point of irrelevance for iPhone users. The Air changes that calculus, at least for this device.

The Air is notably slimmer and lighter than the Whoop, uses LED light patterns and double-tap gestures to show battery status and dismiss alarms, and promises up to a week of battery life. The absence of a screen forces clever interaction design. Google’s solution—haptic feedback and gesture controls—is the same approach Whoop uses, which is either a sign that the problem has one obvious answer, or that no one has found a better one yet.

Google is also allowing users to pair both a Pixel Watch 4 and the Fitbit Air to the same Google Health account simultaneously, enabling a natural split: wear the watch during the day for notifications and navigation, then switch to the Air at night for sleep tracking without the bulk. This is actually a smart use case that Whoop users have cobbled together with their own setups for years—pairing a Whoop with an Apple Watch or Garmin. Google is formalizing it.

The subscription question: the Fitbit Air costs $99.99, which is a stark contrast to Whoop’s $199-a-year subscription model where the hardware is bundled. Google does offer Fitbit Premium (now rebranded Google Health Premium) as an optional upgrade, but core tracking works without it. That’s the right call for mainstream adoption.

Luna Band: The Challenger With a Different Angle

The Luna Band is now fully official with an upgraded design from the prototype shown at CES in January. It’s priced around £115/$149, carries no subscription fee, and has a release date of “end of July 2026”—with Drop 1 being invite-only via waitlist.

Luna is not trying to be a cheaper Fitbit Air. The differentiation is deliberate, and it sits in one feature: voice. The Luna Band uses Siri-powered voice interaction on iOS, allowing users to log meals, emotional context, and request health guidance without opening the app at all. You can tell it you slept badly and it adjusts your recommended workout load. You can log stress events verbally and they factor into your recovery data. There’s currently no word on Android support for the voice layer, which is a significant gap given where most fitness app users live.

The tracker runs on Luna’s proprietary LifeOS operating system, which the company claims processes thousands of physiological signals per minute, detecting changes in recovery, stress, circadian alignment, and hormonal patterns to deliver context-aware recommendations throughout the day. That’s the pitch. Whether LifeOS delivers on it will only be clear once reviewers get sustained time with shipping units.

The Luna Band tracks sleep and fertility alongside the standard metrics, and comes in four colors: Aloe Green, Onyx Black, Desert Beige, and Ember Orange. The app includes “micro-apps” for training, stress, and productivity, and users can build custom health modules.

The $149 price is $50 more than the Fitbit Air, which creates a real question about positioning. Luna is asking buyers to pay more for a device from a brand that launched its smart ring not long ago, on a promise that AI-led voice coaching is worth the premium. For some users—particularly those who find app dashboards overwhelming—that bet might make sense. For users who want established software and a familiar brand, the Fitbit Air is the obvious choice.

Garmin Cirqa: The One Nobody Knows the Price Of Yet

The Garmin Cirqa first appeared on Garmin’s own website in January 2026 before being removed, followed by a trademark filing in February. Recent Garmin Connect app updates confirm the company is actively preparing software support for a screenless device. Every week brings another breadcrumb.

The Cirqa is expected to focus on recovery and readiness tracking—Body Battery, heart rate variability, SpO2, and stress—without GPS or a display, positioned as a companion device for athletes who already wear a Garmin GPS watch during training and want a lighter option for sleep and recovery tracking the rest of the time.

This is a fundamentally different use case than what Google or Luna are targeting. Garmin users are, on average, more serious about training data than the average Fitbit buyer. The Cirqa doesn’t need to convince anyone that recovery metrics matter—it needs to show that Garmin’s implementation of those metrics is better than wearing a Whoop alongside a Fenix. Given that Garmin’s Body Battery algorithm already runs on many of their watches, the Cirqa would essentially offer that same data layer in a form factor people can sleep in.

New Garmin Connect app code suggests the Cirqa may not be exclusively wrist-worn—the app now references wear location setup, hinting at support for body placements beyond the wrist, similar to how Whoop sells its sensor in garments. If that’s true, it would be Garmin’s most significant departure from traditional form factors, and it would give athletes running chest placements or bicep mounts a native option inside the Garmin ecosystem.

No pricing has leaked credibly yet. Given Garmin’s tendency toward premium positioning and the fact that the device targets existing Garmin users already invested in Connect, a price above $100 seems likely.

What This Looks Like for Buyers Right Now

The screenless tracker market in mid-2026 has gone from one dominant player (Whoop) and a few budget alternatives (Amazfit Helio at $99) to a category with genuine choices across price and philosophy.

The comparison that matters most isn’t Fitbit Air versus Luna Band—it’s whether either of them can displace the existing Whoop install base. Whoop users tend to be sticky. The data continuity, the app experience, and the social layer all create switching friction. Someone who’s been tracking HRV trends for two years doesn’t abandon that history lightly.

Where Google and Luna have a real opportunity is with the much larger population that has never bought a screenless tracker. People who looked at Whoop’s subscription price and kept scrolling. That’s the market the Fitbit Air was built for. Luna is betting that within that group, a slice wants an AI coach that talks back.

The Subscription Model Is Losing

The most significant story here isn’t any individual device. It’s that every major challenger entering this category in 2026 is doing so without a mandatory subscription. Luna Band is subscription-free at $149, compared to Whoop’s model where the device is bundled into an annual plan costing $199–$239. Fitbit Air’s core features don’t require one. The Amazfit Helio never had one. Even if Garmin’s Cirqa ends up tied to Connect+, that subscription covers a much broader suite of Garmin services—it won’t feel like you’re paying to unlock a single device.

Whoop built a business on the idea that continuous coaching and data is a service, not a product. The challengers are testing the counter-thesis: that most users want to pay once and own the hardware. The coming year will produce actual data on whether the subscription model was ever the preference or just the only option available.

For anyone in the market right now: the Fitbit Air is the cleanest entry point if you’re starting fresh. It’s $99, works on your phone regardless of platform, ships in days, and Google isn’t going anywhere. The Luna Band is worth the waitlist if voice-led guidance sounds more appealing than a dashboard of numbers. And if you’re already deep in the Garmin ecosystem—especially if you’re wearing a Fenix or Forerunner daily—it’s probably worth waiting another month to see what the Cirqa actually costs.

The category has never had this many credible options at once. That’s worth something, even if it makes the decision harder.
