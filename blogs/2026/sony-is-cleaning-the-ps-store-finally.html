Sony Is Cleaning the PS Store. Finally.

June 25, 2026 10-Minute Read

For years, the PlayStation Store’s “New Releases” section looked less like a curated games catalogue and more like a flea market after a power outage. Hundreds of titles a week. Identical thumbnails. Games with names like The Jumping Burger, Cute Bonfire, Snack and Quack. And if you knew what you were looking at, you understood the business model instantly: ship fast, price low, attach a Platinum Trophy to the thing, and watch a certain subset of PlayStation’s user base open their wallets.

Sony largely let it happen. For years.

That appears to be changing — and in 2026, the change has been dramatic enough to warrant the word “purge.”

The Spark: A Brazilian Studio With 900 Games and a Goodbye Post

The clearest confirmation of Sony’s new posture came on June 23, 2026. Afil Games — a Brazilian studio behind low-effort titles like Collie Call and Cute Bonfire — posted a statement confirming that “PlayStation has been implementing stricter guidelines for publishing games on its platform,” and that its future releases would no longer be available on PS5. All of its previous titles, over 900 of them, would be removed from the PS Store. [Push Square]

Read that number again. Nine hundred titles. From one studio.

Afil Games described its goal as creating “fun, casual, and accessible games” and had released more than 140 games on PS4 and PS5, including titles like Piggy’s Farm, Axobubble, and Slap the Rocks. Some players had accused the studio of producing cheap, low-effort games whose primary value was the PlayStation Platinum Trophy attached to each one — easy enough to earn that completionists would buy them regardless of quality. [Videogames Chronicle]

This is the core mechanism at play, and it explains why shovelware proliferated so aggressively on PlayStation specifically: the Trophy system creates a demand driver that doesn’t exist in the same form on other platforms. Players who care about their trophy level — a visible, public metric on PSN — have an incentive to buy games that are mechanically worthless, simply because they yield a Platinum quickly and cheaply. Shovelware publishers figured this out. They built entire catalogues around it.

What Happened Before Afil: A Timeline of the Purge

Afil’s statement was the first time a publisher confirmed the existence of new guidelines. But the removals themselves had been happening for months. Quietly. Without any official explanation from Sony.

The pattern looks like this:

  • January 2026: ThiGames, which held the fourth-highest title count on the PS Store, had its entire catalog of over 1,196 games silently delisted. ThiGames was behind titles like The Jumping Taco, The Jumping Pizza, and The Jumping Churros — along with dozens of racing and platformer titles that could all be Platinumed in under five minutes.
  • March 2026: CGI Lab and Nostra Games were removed in another quiet sweep.
  • April 2026: GoGame Console Publisher, VRCForge Studios, and Welding Byte were shown the door.
  • May 2026: Testagamecreations had at least 12 games removed — a publisher notorious for releasing obvious clones of popular titles.
  • June 2026: Webnetic announced its PlayStation exit, with communities on Reddit and PSNProfiles also flagging Violarte, SimulaMaker, and West Connection as additional publishers apparently affected. [Gaming ProMax]

Sony moved particularly swiftly in 2026, removing around 1,000 games in January, then again in April, and another batch of around 1,000 earlier in June. [Insider Gaming]

Sony said nothing publicly about any of it. No press release. No developer notice. No policy update. Just — gone.

Why the PS Store Got So Bad in the First Place

To understand the scale of this problem, you need to understand the economics. Submitting to the PlayStation Store was not historically difficult or expensive, and Sony collected a 30% revenue cut on every sale. A publisher who pumps out 900 games priced at $2.99 each is generating meaningful revenue on the platform even with microscopic sales numbers per title. Sony had, in effect, a financial incentive to look the other way.

As of January 2026, the four publishers with the most titles on the PlayStation Store were eastasiasoft with 2,018 games, Ratalaika Games with 1,279, Webnetic with 1,222, and ThiGames with 1,194. Sony itself didn’t land in the top 10, coming in at 11th place with 666 titles. [Outlook Respawn]

Let that settle. The platform holder — the company that makes the console — had fewer games on its own store than publishers best known for asset flips and trophy farms.

The practical effect for regular users was brutal. “New Release” and “Coming Soon” pages became overwhelmed by half-baked $2.99 nonsense, making it genuinely hard to find indie games and new releases. It pushed customers toward sponsored sections and curated selections of the biggest titles — which is, of course, also where the money is — but it squeezed out the middle layer of legitimate independent developers who couldn’t pay for visibility. [Kotaku]

AI tools made it worse. Many of the shovelware titles delisted in March 2026 had relied on generative AI to produce their assets. The PS Store had no clear guidelines against AI-generated content, meaning developers could use the tools to create low-cost knockoffs even faster and at higher volume. [Notebookcheck] Vibe coding — the practice of generating functional game code through AI prompts with minimal human oversight — lowered the barrier even further. The shovel, as one outlet put it, had become motorized.

What “Stricter Guidelines” Actually Means (Nobody Fully Knows)

Here is the uncomfortable part. Sony still hasn’t published a definitive set of criteria for what now gets rejected or delisted. No official criteria have been published. The pattern has fueled speculation that Sony is reviewing publishers with extensive catalogs of low-quality releases, but the actual threshold remains opaque. [GamingHQ]

That opacity creates two distinct problems.

The first is fairness. Some users pointed out that it’s not entirely clear whether Sony would have the legal or ethical right to unilaterally ban a publisher’s entire catalog on the basis that those games “kinda sucked.” This led to speculation that some publishers must have done something more specifically egregious — a Terms of Service violation, perhaps — rather than simply failing a quality bar that was never formally communicated. [Gizmodo]

The second is consistency. Publishers like Eastasiasoft and Ratalaika Games — who hold the top two spots for title count on PSN — are widely considered legitimate operations that serve a genuine audience of players who enjoy short, affordable games. They’re routinely listed alongside ThiGames in the same spreadsheets. If Sony’s enforcement is blunt rather than precise, real studios with real audiences will eventually get caught in the net.

Sony has previously removed the most notorious shovelware developers only after significant public pressure, with many of them reportedly returning under new names. Players had long called for a system to filter out low-effort content at submission, rather than removing games after the fact. [PlayStation LifeStyle] What Afil’s statement suggests — and it’s the first real evidence of this — is that the filter may now be moving upstream, into the publishing approval process itself. That’s a meaningful shift. But without transparency about what the guidelines actually say, it’s impossible to evaluate whether they’re being applied fairly.

The Trophy System Problem Nobody Wants to Fix

There is a deeper structural issue here that no amount of delistings will resolve: the PlayStation Trophy system is the engine that drives demand for shovelware, and Sony has shown no inclination to change it.

Some trophy hunters enjoyed these games as easy ways to earn PlayStation trophies, while others argue that removing titles after purchase availability raises legitimate concerns about preservation and consumer choice. [GamingHQ]

The trophy hunting community is not monolithic. A significant portion of it is built around genuine mastery — 100%-completing games like Returnal or Sekiro represents real skill and real time investment. But a parallel subset exists that treats trophy level as a pure number to be maximized, and that subset created the market that shovelware publishers were serving.

Sony’s move addresses the supply side of that equation. It’s removing the cheap Platinum games. But as long as a player’s PSN profile publicly displays a trophy count that can be inflated by pressing X until a burger jumps high enough, demand-side pressure will look for new outlets — on Xbox, on Switch, on Steam, wherever the next low-friction Platinum equivalent can be found.

Afil Games noted it would continue supporting players on Xbox and Nintendo Switch [GameDev.net] — which is exactly what you’d expect. The content doesn’t disappear. It migrates.

What This Means for Legitimate Indie Developers

The optimistic read is that Sony’s action is straightforwardly good for the independent development ecosystem. When dozens of near-identical $2.99 titles flood the new releases queue every day, they don’t just annoy players — they structurally disadvantage developers who spent meaningful time building something original.

Discoverability on a platform store is largely a function of momentum. A game that sells in its first few days tends to surface in recommendation algorithms and “new release” lists, generating more visibility, generating more sales. When the storefront is overwhelmed with low-effort titles, that momentum becomes harder to build — interesting opportunities get buried, and players default to the sections Sony explicitly curates. [Kotaku] Reducing the volume of junk at the submission stage — if that’s what Sony is now doing — directly benefits the developers who can least afford to pay for placement.

The pessimistic read is that Sony still hasn’t told anyone the rules. An independent developer who works quickly, releases frequently, or makes simple games by choice could theoretically find themselves caught in a policy sweep they never saw coming. Until Sony publishes clear guidelines, the “stricter” standards remain a black box — which is not a great operating environment for small studios making legitimate decisions about their release strategies.

Six Months In, the Purge Is Real. But Incomplete.

There are still plenty of low-effort games on the PS Store, and it looks like Sony will get around to addressing more of them over time — but it’s been a slow process. [PlayStation LifeStyle]

That’s a fair characterization. The publishers removed so far were, by most accounts, the most egregious cases — studios with hundreds or thousands of near-identical titles built almost entirely around the Trophy system, often leaning on AI tools to generate assets at industrial scale. The harder curation work lies ahead: drawing defensible lines between a low-budget game made with care and a low-budget game made with contempt.

Sony’s silence on its own process is the clearest signal that it doesn’t yet have a clean answer to that question.

What’s not in doubt is that something real is happening. Tens of thousands of titles have been removed from Sony’s storefront in 2026 alone. [Push Square] A publisher has now gone on record confirming the existence of stricter guidelines. The delistings are not random or isolated — they’re accelerating, they’re targeting the same profile of publisher each time, and they’re affecting studios across multiple countries.

The PlayStation Store is getting cleaner. Slowly, imprecisely, and without anything like the transparency the situation warrants — but it’s getting cleaner. Whether Sony formalizes the process before it causes collateral damage to legitimate developers is the question worth watching now.
