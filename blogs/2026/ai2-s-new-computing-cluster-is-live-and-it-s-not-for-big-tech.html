Ai2's New Computing Cluster Is Live—and It's Not for Big Tech

May 9, 2026 8-Minute Read

The compute gap between academic researchers and commercial AI labs has been widening for years. Training a competitive large language model now costs tens of millions of dollars in GPU time alone—resources that universities and nonprofit institutes simply don’t have. That’s the problem the Allen Institute for AI set out to address, and on May 7, 2026, it reached a concrete milestone: Ai2 brought online a new Nvidia-powered computing system, the first major deliverable in a $152 million project to build open AI models for scientific research. [GeekWire]

This isn’t a press release cluster—it’s already running real experiments.

What OMAI Actually Is

The project, called the Open Multimodal AI Infrastructure for Science (NSF OMAI), is backed by the U.S. National Science Foundation and Nvidia, whose Blackwell Ultra GPUs power the new infrastructure. Ai2 designed the cluster to develop a fully open AI ecosystem aimed at accelerating discovery in fields like materials science, biology, and energy. [Substack]

NSF contributed $75 million while Nvidia put in an additional $77 million, channeled through a Mid-Scale Research Infrastructure award. [NSF - National Science Foundation] The project runs five years. Dr. Noah A. Smith, Ai2’s senior director of NLP research and Amazon Professor of Machine Learning at the University of Washington, is the principal investigator.

Partner institutions include the University of Washington, the University of Hawai’i at Hilo, the University of New Hampshire, and the University of New Mexico. [Allen AI] This isn’t a single-lab effort—it’s a distributed academic coalition with shared infrastructure at its center.

The Hardware: Austin, Blackwell Ultra, and a Familiar Partner

The new system is located outside of Austin and runs on Nvidia’s Blackwell Ultra chips. It is managed by Cirrascale Cloud Services. [GeekWire]

Cirrascale is not a random vendor pick. Cirrascale is the infrastructure platform provider that Ai2 already partners with to distribute its OLMo, Molmo, and Tulu AI models. [Ken Yeung] The relationship predates OMAI—which means the operational playbook for deploying and scaling Ai2’s model families is already established. That continuity matters. Spinning up a new compute cluster with an unfamiliar managed services provider is its own form of technical debt. Ai2 avoided that here.

Nvidia’s contribution also goes beyond cutting a check. The company is providing its HGX B3-100 systems, built on the Blackwell Ultra architecture, along with its AI Enterprise software stack. [Jon Peddie Research] The Blackwell Ultra generation represents a significant step up in memory bandwidth and transformer throughput—exactly what you need when training models across text, images, and structured scientific data simultaneously.

What “Fully Open” Means Here—and Why It’s Different

Plenty of AI projects use the word “open.” Few mean the same thing by it.

Unlike most large-scale AI projects, Ai2 releases the full code, data, and training methods behind its models, allowing other researchers to reproduce and build on the work. [GeekWire] That’s not just weights on Hugging Face—it’s the entire pipeline: pretraining data, intermediate checkpoints, post-training recipes, evaluation code.

In closed systems, compute spent on experiments and iteration typically yields a single commercial product. However, when that same work is made open, Ai2 argues it continues to generate value “long after training ends.” [Ken Yeung] It’s a compounding model of research output rather than a one-time release. Each training run becomes a citable, reproducible artifact that other labs can fork and study.

One Oxford researcher described OLMo as uniquely enabling their work precisely because it is fully open source—offering an open and credible platform where the community can investigate fundamental questions about reasoning, bias, fairness, and trustworthiness, in a landscape dominated by commercially optimized systems. [Allen AI] That kind of testimonial from academia is hard to manufacture. It comes from the fact that full openness is genuinely rare at this scale.

Results Already—Before the Cluster Hit Full Capacity

This is worth slowing down on: the cluster came online recently, and Ai2 already has publishable outputs.

Molmo 2 introduced video understanding, pointing, and object tracking to Ai2’s multimodal model family—with an 8B-parameter model surpassing the original 72B Molmo on key benchmarks. A follow-up release, MolmoPoint, replaced text-coordinate outputs with a token-based grounding mechanism tied directly to the model’s visual features, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy on spatial reasoning tasks. [Substack]

The jump from 72B to 8B while improving benchmarks isn’t just efficiency—it’s a different kind of signal. Smaller, more capable models are more deployable in constrained research environments. A university lab running protein folding experiments doesn’t need a 70-billion-parameter model eating up every available GPU. An 8B model that outperforms it is actually more useful in the real world.

On the language side, OLMo Hybrid combined transformer attention with linear RNN layers, matching prior models while using roughly half the training data. [Substack] Halving training data requirements matters enormously at scale—it’s the difference between a training run that takes weeks and one that takes months, and between a budget that sustains a five-year project and one that doesn’t.

The Nonprofit Angle in a Commercial AI Landscape

Ai2 was founded in 2014 by the late Paul G. Allen with a mandate to do open, high-impact research. The organization has consistently released models, datasets, and research tools without restrictions—a track record that made it the natural anchor for a project whose entire premise is that openness is a feature, not a concession. [Granted AI]

That history is doing real work here. NSF didn’t fund a generic AI consortium—it picked an institution with a decade of demonstrated commitment to releasing research artifacts that other researchers can actually use. That’s not easy to fake, and it’s not something a commercial lab can credibly claim even if it wanted to.

The cluster is built around the philosophy that openness and reuse matter more than the sheer size of its computing footprint—prioritizing how effectively capacity is used and shared. [Substack]

The comparison to commercial AI development is instructive. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind—each runs infrastructure that costs orders of magnitude more. But the output of those systems is largely proprietary. When Ai2 trains a model, the training run itself becomes a scientific contribution.

The Institutional Context: A Lab Under Pressure

None of this is happening in a vacuum. The announcement came as Ai2 works to regain its footing after losing its CEO and some of its top researchers to Microsoft in March 2026. [GeekWire]

Interim CEO Peter Clark outlined Ai2’s priorities this week, saying the institute is committed to open models and longer-term research, along with applied AI efforts in areas such as scientific discovery and environmental science. [GeekWire]

Going live on a $152 million federally backed computing cluster in the middle of a leadership transition is a statement. It signals that the institutional machinery—the research programs, the funding relationships, the infrastructure partnerships—is durable enough to survive organizational turbulence. Whether that stability holds as Ai2 searches for permanent leadership is a different question. But for now, the cluster is running, models are shipping, and the project is tracking to its goals.

What Comes Next

Ai2 said it is now focused on building unified models that handle multiple types of data, developing AI agents, and working more closely with scientific communities to ensure the models are useful for real-world research. [GeekWire]

Some of this work has already surfaced in the form of its Open Coding Agents family and MolmoWeb, alongside ongoing research into how training strategies and environments shape reliable agentic behavior. [Allen AI]

The five-year timeline gives OMAI room to grow into something genuinely different from anything the commercial AI world is building. Not because the hardware is bigger—it isn’t—but because the output is designed to be reused, studied, and extended rather than monetized. That’s a different optimization target, and it produces a different kind of science.

Why Academic AI Infrastructure Is a National Security Question

NSF’s acting director framed the investment explicitly in terms of securing U.S. global leadership in science and technology, and tackling challenges once thought impossible. [NVIDIA Blog]

That framing is deliberate. The compute gap doesn’t just disadvantage researchers—it shapes which questions get asked. When only well-capitalized commercial labs can afford to train frontier models, the research agenda tilts toward commercially viable applications. Drug discovery, materials synthesis, climate modeling—these are areas where AI could be transformative, but where the payoff timelines are too long for a venture-funded product roadmap.

OMAI is a bet that public investment can keep those questions alive. The Blackwell Ultra cluster outside Austin is the physical infrastructure behind that bet. It’s now online, and early results suggest it’s working.
