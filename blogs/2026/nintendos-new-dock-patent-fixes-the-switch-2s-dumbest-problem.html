Nintendo's New Dock Patent Fixes the Switch 2's Dumbest Problem

July 17, 2026 5-Minute Read

You bought a Switch 2. You still own an original Switch. Now you own two docks, because Nintendo’s new hardware won’t charge or display video through your old one, and vice versa. That’s the reality right now, and it’s exactly the kind of small, avoidable annoyance that makes multi-console households mutter under their breath every time they swap systems on the TV.

A newly surfaced Nintendo patent, filed in China and spotted by the account Nintendo Patents Watch on Bluesky, describes a dock that would fix this. It automatically detects whether you’ve plugged in a first-generation Switch or a Switch 2, then reconfigures itself on the fly. Video resolution, USB data speed, and fan behavior all shift depending on which console just slid into the slot.

Why Your Current Dock Can’t Just “Figure It Out”

The reason this is even a problem comes down to power delivery, not stubbornness on Nintendo’s part. The original Switch tops out at an 18-watt charging draw. The Switch 2 is rated for up to 60 watts. Those are two very different electrical conversations happening between console and dock, and the negotiation protocol has to match or nothing charges safely.

That mismatch is also why plugging an old Switch into a Switch 2 dock doesn’t work today. The dock sends more voltage than the original console expects, and the two devices essentially refuse to talk. It’s not a software oversight. It’s a hardware safety boundary.

The patented dock solves this by detecting the connected device first, the way a phone charger senses whether it’s plugged into a laptop or a wall brick, and only then deciding how much power to send.

What Actually Changes When You Swap Consoles

Nintendo’s filing lays out three things that shift automatically based on which Switch is plugged in.

  • Video output. The Switch 2 gets a higher-resolution signal sent to the display. The original Switch, which was never built for 4K, receives a lower-resolution signal instead. This isn’t the dock “downgrading” anything. It’s matching output to what the connected hardware can actually process.
  • USB data mode. The Switch 2 activates USB 3.0 for faster data transfer. The older console drops back to USB 2.0, which is the standard it was designed around.
  • Fan speed. An internal fan spins faster for the Switch 2 and slower for the original Switch, based on each console’s cooling needs. The Switch 2 runs hotter under docked play, so it needs the extra airflow. The original Switch mostly doesn’t.

None of these adjustments are exotic engineering. They’re the same kind of “detect and adapt” logic you already see in universal phone chargers or multi-device USB hubs. The genuinely new part is Nintendo building it specifically to bridge two generations of its own hardware, something the company rarely bothers doing.

Don’t Get Attached to This Yet, and Here’s Why

Patents get filed constantly, and most of them never become products sitting on a shelf. Companies patent defensively, patent early ideas that get scrapped, and patent things purely to keep competitors from doing it first. This particular filing was submitted in January 2025, six months before the Switch 2 even launched [HotHardware] , which tells you Nintendo was thinking about backward compatibility for the dock well before the console shipped, yet chose not to ship that feature in the actual retail dock.

That gap between “patented” and “released” matters. It means the idea existed early enough to make the cut, and Nintendo decided against it anyway, at least for now.

There’s a second reason to stay cautious: the patent doesn’t appear to address VRR, or variable refresh rate, which is one of the more persistent complaints about the current official dock. A recent Nintendo job listing referenced ongoing research into potential fixes for VRR support [Notebookcheck] , suggesting that if a next dock revision does happen, it might be solving a different problem than the one this patent describes.

What This Signals About Nintendo’s Actual Plans for the Old Switch

The more interesting takeaway isn’t the dock itself. It’s what the patent implies about how long Nintendo intends to keep the original Switch alive as a supported product. A company doesn’t spend engineering time solving cross-generation charging and video output for a console it plans to abandon.

That lines up with other signals. Outside of Europe, Nintendo has given no indication it plans to stop supporting the original Switch [Notebookcheck] , even with the successor console now firmly established. A universal dock would make sense as an accessory for households easing that transition rather than making a hard switch overnight, letting one dock serve a family where a kid still plays the original Switch while a parent has moved to the new one.

Should You Wait to Buy a Second Dock

If you’re currently juggling two docks and wondering whether to hold off on buying a second official one, here’s the honest answer: don’t wait on a patent. Patents can sit dormant for years, and there’s a real chance this specific design never leaves the filing cabinet. The six-month gap between filing and Switch 2 launch, combined with Nintendo choosing not to include this functionality in the retail dock, suggests the company had reasons to shelve it, whether that’s cost, complexity, or a decision to revisit the idea later.

Third-party docks already claim cross-generation compatibility today, some advertising support for both consoles alongside devices like the Steam Deck or ROG Ally. Those are worth considering if the two-dock hassle is bothering you right now, since Nintendo’s own fix, if it ever ships, likely won’t arrive for a long while. If it does happen to materialize, it would probably show up as a running-change revision to the existing dock rather than a new standalone product, which is how Nintendo has historically handled quiet hardware updates.
