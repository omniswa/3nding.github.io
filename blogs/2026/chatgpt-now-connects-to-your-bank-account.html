ChatGPT Now Connects to Your Bank Account

May 17, 2026 10-Minute Read

Most budgeting tools have a version of the same problem: they know what you spent, but they don’t know what you’re trying to do. You get charts. You get categories. You get a pie slice that tells you dining out is 23% of your monthly expenses—as if you didn’t already feel vaguely guilty about that. What you rarely get is a conversation about whether any of it matters given your actual goals, your income trajectory, your upcoming rent increase, or the car you’re thinking about buying next spring.

That’s the gap OpenAI is trying to close. On May 15, 2026, the company launched a personal finance experience inside ChatGPT for Pro subscribers in the United States, allowing users to connect bank accounts, investment portfolios, credit cards, and other financial accounts directly to the chatbot. The premise is simple: instead of asking generic money questions and getting generic answers, you ask questions about your money.

What It Actually Does

The feature runs on GPT-5.5, OpenAI’s latest reasoning model, and connects to financial institutions through Plaid—the same financial data infrastructure that powers apps like Venmo, Robinhood, and most modern budgeting tools. Users can connect to over 12,000 financial institutions, including Schwab, Fidelity, Chase, Robinhood, American Express, and Capital One.

Once accounts are linked, ChatGPT shows a dashboard with an up-to-date view of portfolio performance, spending, subscriptions, upcoming payments, and more. Beyond the dashboard, users can ask questions anchored in their real transaction data. OpenAI gives examples like “I feel like I’ve been spending more recently—what changed?” or “Help me build a plan to buy a house in my area in the next five years.”

Those are genuinely different questions from what you could ask before. The first one requires access to your transaction history. The second one needs to know your income, your current savings rate, your existing debts, and some sense of local real estate prices. A generic chatbot can sketch out frameworks for both. A chatbot with your financial data can start running actual numbers.

Beyond connected accounts, users can share additional context about their financial lives—a mortgage, a savings goal, or a major purchase they’re planning—and ChatGPT can save that context to “Financial memories” to inform future conversations. So if you tell it you owe your parents money, it can factor that into its thinking the next time you ask about cash flow.

To get started, users can select “Get started” in the “Finances” option in the sidebar, or type “@Finances, connect my accounts” in a ChatGPT conversation. The chatbot then guides them through linking accounts through Plaid.

The Moves That Got OpenAI Here

This didn’t come out of nowhere. In October 2025, OpenAI quietly acquired personal finance app Roi, bringing in that startup’s co-founder and CEO. Then, in April 2026, OpenAI acquired personal finance startup Hiro Finance, founded by Ethan Bloch—a serial fintech entrepreneur who previously built Digit, a savings app sold to Oportun in 2021 for more than $200 million.

Hiro billed itself as an “AI personal CFO” and said it helped clients manage more than $1 billion in assets. The acquisition was structured as an acqui-hire—Hiro shut down its product, deleted user data, and the team moved to OpenAI. Within a month, a finance feature landed in ChatGPT.

That timing matters. According to CCG Catalyst managing partner Paul Schaus, the purpose of the deal was not to integrate an existing product—it was about “bringing a team and a founder who knows how to build consumer finance tools that people use.” Bloch’s background in financial math precision (Hiro was specifically trained to get financial calculations right) was likely as valuable as anything else he brought over. One of the recurring criticisms of general-purpose AI in finance has been accuracy: rounding errors and confident-sounding wrong numbers are particularly dangerous in a domain where $200 versus $2,000 is the difference between a manageable credit card bill and a serious problem.

What ChatGPT Can and Cannot Do With Your Money

This is where the practical limits matter more than the marketing.

ChatGPT can see balances, transactions, investments, and liabilities, but it cannot see full account numbers or make changes to accounts. It cannot move money, pay bills, place trades, or file your taxes. OpenAI is explicit that this tool does not replace professional financial advice.

The Intuit integration, which is coming soon, will add one meaningful capability: it would enable analysis such as the impact of a stock sale on taxes or the odds of a credit card approval. That’s a more sophisticated use case than spending categorization. Tax-loss harvesting scenarios, Roth conversion analysis, the interaction between selling appreciated shares and your effective marginal rate—those are genuinely hard to think through without a tool that can hold multiple variables at once.

But even with Intuit connected, ChatGPT is still an advisor in the loosest sense. It can model scenarios. It cannot execute them. For someone who just wants clarity on why their checking account keeps running low by the 25th of the month, that’s probably enough. For someone managing a more complex portfolio, it’s a useful starting point for a conversation—with an actual advisor—not an endpoint.

The Privacy Tradeoff, Plainly Stated

This is the part of the conversation that deserves more than a paragraph buried at the end of an announcement post.

OpenAI has confirmed that users can disconnect accounts from Settings > Apps > Finances. After disconnection, OpenAI says it removes synced financial data from ChatGPT within 30 days. Users can also view and delete financial memories linked to goals or obligations.

Regarding model training, this information will likely be saved by OpenAI and used to train its AI models if users opt into the “improve the model for everyone” option in settings—a setting that is auto-enabled for users, though easily toggled off.

That’s not a scandal. It’s how most consumer AI products work. But the stakes are meaningfully higher when the data involved is your transaction history rather than your restaurant recommendations. Financial data is specific, persistent, and can be used to infer things people haven’t explicitly disclosed—household size, health conditions, relationship status, income variability. The aggregated version of that data, across millions of users, has obvious commercial value well beyond model training.

OpenAI has not fully explained how it may use aggregated financial data beyond model training, nor has it detailed all extra protections against a data breach. That’s not a reason to avoid the product, but it is a reason to think about what you’re connecting and why. Linking one checking account for spending visibility is different from linking every brokerage account you own.

How It Compares to What Already Exists

The honest comparison here is with tools like Monarch Money, YNAB, and Copilot—apps that have spent years solving exactly this problem and have built meaningful audiences doing it.

Monarch, for instance, connects to Plaid, shows a real-time financial dashboard, and lets you annotate transactions and set goals. Copilot has a cleaner iOS interface than most and surfaces subscription changes automatically. YNAB has a specific methodology around zero-based budgeting that some people find genuinely transformative. All of them cost money—typically $8–$15 per month—and all of them are purpose-built for this use case.

ChatGPT’s advantage is the conversational layer. The ability to ask “what would happen to my savings rate if I refinanced at current rates?” and get a contextual answer using your actual numbers—not a generic calculator—is genuinely new for a mass-market product. The existing apps can show you data. They struggle to reason about it with you.

The disadvantage is the opposite of specialization. A purpose-built budgeting app has years of edge-case handling: what happens when a transaction miscategorizes, how it handles joint accounts, how it deals with manual adjustments. ChatGPT is a general-purpose system being applied to a domain that rewards precision and consistency. Those are different engineering problems.

The core shift is that ChatGPT moves from offering category-level advice (“spend less on dining”) to specific, contextualized insights based on your real transaction history. Whether that shift is durable—whether the system is accurate and reliable enough to trust with decisions that actually affect your financial life—is something only extended use will answer.

Who This Is Actually For Right Now

The feature requires ChatGPT Pro, which costs $100 per month. OpenAI’s $20/month ChatGPT Plus subscription doesn’t support the new personal finance experience yet.

At $100 per month, the audience is self-selected. These are people already paying a significant premium for ChatGPT’s capabilities, likely using it heavily for work or research, and willing to experiment with new features. It’s a reasonable testing group: engaged, technically comfortable, and probably less likely to panic if something miscategorizes a transaction.

OpenAI has confirmed plans to expand the feature to Plus users after collecting feedback from the Pro preview, with no specific timeline announced. Given OpenAI’s cadence on other feature rollouts, a Plus expansion in Q3 or Q4 2026 is a reasonable expectation—but not confirmed.

The more interesting question is what this looks like when it reaches the full ChatGPT user base. More than 200 million people already come to ChatGPT every month for budgeting questions, investment questions, and financial planning. Most of them are asking without any connected data—getting advice calibrated to nothing specific about their situation. If even a fraction of them connect accounts, OpenAI will be handling financial data at a scale that no dedicated personal finance app has ever approached.

What This Signals About Where ChatGPT Is Heading

A Javelin analyst described the deal this way: “The deal is less about OpenAI entering banking and more about what industry will own financial advice and engagement.” That framing is probably the right one.

OpenAI is not trying to become a bank or a brokerage. It is trying to become the interface layer between people and their financial lives—the place you go when you have a money question, whether that question is “where is all my money going” or “should I pay down debt or invest the difference.” Banks and fintechs have historically owned that relationship. Personal finance apps have chipped away at it. ChatGPT, with 200 million monthly users already asking financial questions, is making a more direct move.

The health analogy is instructive. Both OpenAI and Anthropic have launched health-related tools, and AI companies are increasingly realizing that generalist chatbots are being used for sensitive personal domains—finance, health, relationships—and moving to build more structured experiences for those use cases. Finance is a natural next step: high stakes, high anxiety, and chronically underserved for people who can’t afford a private wealth manager.

Whether ChatGPT is the tool that finally gets people to understand their own finances is an open question. The technology is plausibly capable of it. The data connection is real. The remaining gap is trust—and trust in this context means accuracy, privacy, and consistency over time, not just a clean dashboard and a compelling demo.

That’s a gap no single product launch closes. But as first steps go, this one is more considered than most.
