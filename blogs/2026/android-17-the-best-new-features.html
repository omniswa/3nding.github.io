Android 17: The Best New Features

May 14, 2026 11-Minute Read

Most Android updates follow a predictable rhythm: a redesigned settings menu, a few camera tweaks, maybe a new gesture or two. Android 17 does not feel like that. Google’s 2026 release is the first in a long time where the underlying architecture has changed in ways that will affect how the phone actually behaves, not just how it looks. The best new features of Android 17 span AI automation, security, multitasking, and battery management—and several of them are the kinds of things Android users have been asking for since at least 2022.

The stable release is expected in June or July 2026, arriving first on Pixel and Samsung Galaxy S26 devices. Here’s what’s worth paying attention to.

Gemini Intelligence: The Phone Actually Does Things for You

The headline feature of Android 17 is Gemini Intelligence, and Google isn’t calling it a feature—they’re positioning it as a reclassification of what the OS is. The pitch is that Android is now an “intelligence system” rather than just an operating system.

That’s marketing language, but the underlying capability is real. Gemini can now complete multi-step tasks autonomously across apps. You pull up a grocery list in Google Keep, long-press the power button, and Gemini builds a shopping cart in your preferred delivery app. You take a photo of a travel brochure and ask it to find and book a trip for four people—it handles the search, applies filters, and presents the checkout before you confirm. It runs these tasks in the background, sends progress notifications, and requires your approval before any purchase goes through.

This is a meaningful shift from how AI assistants have worked until now, which was mostly reactive and single-app. The agentic capability runs across Google-connected apps as well as Chrome on the web, meaning it can also handle things like parking reservations or appointment booking through browser interfaces.

There is a real caveat worth naming: Google hasn’t published a full breakdown of which tasks run on-device versus in the cloud. Gemini Nano handles some local processing, but the complex multi-app automation almost certainly requires a connection. For users in areas with patchy connectivity, or anyone with strong privacy preferences, that matters. Every AI feature is opt-in, and you can enable app automation on a per-app basis, which is the right design choice—but don’t assume this is all happening privately on your device.

Intelligent Autofill and Rambler

Two smaller pieces of Gemini Intelligence that will see heavy daily use are Intelligent Autofill and Rambler.

Intelligent Autofill uses context Gemini has already gathered—from Google Photos, Gmail, Google Wallet, and your past activity—to fill out forms accurately. Keep a photo of your passport in Google Photos; Gemini can extract the relevant fields and autofill them when you’re booking a flight. It knows your home address, your card details, and enough about your preferences to make form-filling feel genuinely different from what autofill has been until now.

Rambler is a new Gboard dictation mode that doesn’t transcribe speech verbatim—it summarizes it. If you think out loud when composing a message, talking through half a story before arriving at your actual point, Rambler distills that into readable text without you having to edit it afterward. It’s a quiet but genuinely useful upgrade for anyone who dictates frequently.

Create My Widget: Custom Widgets With Plain Language

This one stands out because it solves a real, longstanding frustration without requiring any technical skill.

Android has always had better widget support than iOS in theory, but in practice you were limited to whatever the developer of a given app chose to build. If the widget you wanted didn’t exist, your options were to use a third-party widget pack, learn KWGT, or give up. Neither option felt like something that should require workarounds on the most customizable mobile OS on the market.

With Android 17, you can describe the widget you want in plain text and the OS generates it. “Show me my three most recent unread emails with sender names” or “display today’s weather with the hourly forecast in a two-column layout”—the system creates a working widget from the description. For large-screen devices and foldables especially, where the home screen real estate makes widgets far more useful, this changes the customization ceiling significantly.

App Bubbles for Everything

Bubbles—the floating app overlay system—used to be limited to messaging apps. Android 17 extends it to any app on any device. Long-press a launcher icon and you can float it as a bubble over whatever you’re currently doing.

The practical case is straightforward: watching a YouTube video in full screen while keeping a Google Keep bubble accessible for notes, or checking a recipe in a bubble while navigating a map in the main view. On tablets and foldables, there’s a dedicated bubble bar in the taskbar for organizing multiple active bubbles.

This isn’t a revolutionary concept—Samsung DeX and desktop-class multitasking have blurred the lines here for years—but having it as a native system feature, available across all Android 17 devices regardless of OEM, is a meaningful baseline upgrade.

Security Changes That Actually Close Dangerous Gaps

Android 17 may represent the most substantive security overhaul Google has shipped in a single release. Several of these changes address gaps that were genuinely exploitable in prior versions.

OTP hiding is now on by default. One-time passwords are automatically hidden from most apps for three hours after you receive them. This closes a real attack surface: malicious apps that quietly read incoming SMS messages—either to hijack account logins or to intercept 2FA codes—can no longer do so immediately after an OTP arrives. Developers need to transition to SMS Retriever or SMS User Consent APIs.

Live threat detection uses on-device AI to flag apps that are behaving suspiciously—forwarding SMS content, abusing accessibility features, hiding their icon and running in the background. This launches in the second half of 2026 as part of Android 17, with rules that can be updated dynamically to respond to new threats without requiring a full OS update.

Advanced Protection mode is receiving its most significant expansion yet. It now blocks non-accessibility apps from using accessibility access entirely—one of the most common vectors for malware on Android. It also disables device-to-device unlocking and adds scam detection for chat notifications. If your organization manages devices, Android Enterprise support for Advanced Protection is also coming later in 2026.

Theft protection defaults are changing globally. Remote Lock and Theft Detection Lock are now enabled by default on all new Android 17 devices and freshly reset phones. Previously these required manual activation. There are also expanded limits on PIN-guessing attempts, with longer wait times between failed entries—a change that makes brute-force physical attacks on a stolen phone significantly harder.

The OTP protection is worth pausing on because it’s one of those changes that seems small until you think about how many people have had accounts compromised by SMS intercept attacks. Making this default behavior rather than a setting users have to find is the correct decision.

Priority Charging

This one has a caveat: as of Android 17 Beta 4, Priority Charging is still marked as a hidden feature and may not ship in the initial stable release. It’s expected either at launch or via a QPR feature drop later in 2026. That said, it’s worth knowing about.

Priority Charging is a mode that, when activated, pauses resource-heavy background processes and limits CPU usage while your phone is plugged in. The idea is that more current goes directly to the battery instead of being consumed by apps refreshing in the background. It’s most effective with a 30W or higher charger. For anyone who charges primarily overnight or during shorter windows, the appeal is obvious—less time waiting for a full charge on days when your schedule is compressed.

It’s a more direct solution than the “charge to 80%” or “charge slowly overnight” features that currently exist, which trade speed for long-term battery health. This one trades processing availability for charging speed, which is a different—and for some users, more useful—tradeoff.

Quick Share Goes Cross-Platform

Quick Share, Android’s file-sharing tool, can now send files to iPhones. Any Android phone running Android 17 can generate a QR code inside Quick Share and send files to an iPhone via the cloud—even without direct device-to-device interoperability. Later in 2026, Quick Share integration is also coming inside WhatsApp and other apps.

Separately, Google and Apple rebuilt the wireless migration tool for switching from iPhone to Android. The updated version transfers passwords, photos, messages, apps, contacts, home screen layout, and eSIM data without a cable. It’s launching on Samsung Galaxy and Google Pixel first.

Neither of these is a flashy feature, but they address something that has been a real friction point. Android-to-iPhone interoperability has historically required workarounds that felt like the OS was barely tolerating the task. Making this native and seamless changes the calculus for people who switch devices regularly or work in mixed-platform households.

Digital Wellbeing: Pause Point

Pause Point is a new Digital Wellbeing tool that adds a 10-second pause screen when you open an app you’ve flagged as distracting. During that 10-second window, you can choose to proceed, do a brief breathing exercise, set a timer for how long you’ll spend in the app, look at a designated photo (a meaningful one you choose), or redirect to an alternative app like an audiobook.

The design detail that matters: turning Pause Point off requires a phone restart. That’s intentional friction. You can’t just swipe away a popup and ignore the nudge—disabling it entirely is a decision that takes a moment of deliberate action. Google says this is the first of several wellbeing features planned for later in 2026.

This is a thoughtful implementation of an idea that has historically been executed poorly. Most screen-time features are trivially easy to bypass, which means they mostly serve as gentle suggestions for people who didn’t need reminders in the first place. Making the friction asymmetric—easy to use, hard to permanently dismiss—is a smarter design.

Screen Reactions for Creators

Screen Reactions is a native Android tool that captures your screen and front camera simultaneously, producing a reaction-style video overlay without a green screen, a second app, or any editing. For creators who build content around reaction videos or commentary, this eliminates a step that previously required third-party tools or at minimum a capable video editing workflow.

It’s paired with improved Instagram support for Android tablets, higher-quality upload pipelines through a new Google-Meta partnership that also brings Ultra HDR capture and Night Mode to Instagram, and an incoming Adobe Premiere app built natively for Android. Google is clearly trying to move professional creative workflows onto Android phones and away from the assumption that content editing happens on a desktop.

What Android 17 Doesn’t Deliver Yet

The search bar on the Pixel Launcher—one of the most requested customization changes for years—is still not removable in the initial Android 17 stable release. Strings found in Android 17 QPR1 beta builds confirm Google is working on a toggle, but it’s expected to land in September 2026 with the QPR1 feature drop, not at launch.

Desktop mode has also received attention in Android 17 development, with improvements aimed at making it compete more directly with Samsung DeX-style experiences. But Google hasn’t fully committed to a polished, widely promoted desktop interface in the way Samsung has. For users who want a proper convergence story—plug in a monitor and get a usable desktop—that’s still mostly a third-party or Samsung-specific story.

Release Timeline and Device Eligibility

Android 17’s stable release is expected in June or July 2026. Google Pixel devices—starting with the Pixel 6 series and newer—will get it first, followed by Samsung Galaxy S26 devices. Samsung’s broader One UI 9 rollout based on Android 17 is expected to launch alongside the Galaxy Z Fold 8 and Z Flip 8 foldables, tentatively in July 2026.

OnePlus, Xiaomi, OPPO, vivo, and HONOR are all in Android 17 preview testing programs, with consumer rollouts expected in the second half of 2026. Galaxy S22 and S21 series devices, along with Pixel 5 and older, are not expected to receive the update.

The Gemini Intelligence agentic features will roll out in waves—beginning with the latest Galaxy and Pixel hardware—before expanding to Android watches, Android Auto, and glasses later in the year.

Android 17 is worth the anticipation. Not because every feature is perfect, and not because the AI integration will work flawlessly for everyone out of the gate, but because the changes are structural rather than cosmetic. Gemini Intelligence, the security overhaul, universal app bubbles, and Create My Widget are the kinds of additions that change actual daily behavior—not just the number in the settings menu.
