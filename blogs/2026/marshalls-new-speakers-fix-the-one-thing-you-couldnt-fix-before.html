Marshall's New Speakers Fix the One Thing You Couldn't Fix Before

July 7, 2026 6-Minute Read

For four years, if the grille on your Marshall speaker cracked or a control knob snapped off, you had two options: live with it, or throw out a $300 speaker over a $2 part. That’s over now.

Marshall just released the Acton IV and Stanmore IV, and the headline isn’t the sound. It’s that the company finally designed these things to be fixed instead of replaced.

The Short Answer, If You’re Deciding Right Now

The Acton IV costs $299.99 and has a 4-inch woofer. The Stanmore IV costs $399.99 and steps up to a 5-inch woofer for bigger rooms. Both landed today, July 7, 2026, replacing speakers that hadn’t been updated in roughly four years. Both now redesign the bass port for punchier low-end, and both let you swap out damaged parts like the grille, knobs, and feet instead of scrapping the whole unit.

If you already own an Acton III or Stanmore III and it still works fine, you don’t need this upgrade. If yours is aging, cracked, or you’re buying your first Marshall speaker, this is the one to get.

Why the Bass Actually Changed, Not Just the Marketing Copy

Marshall redesigned the bass port on both speakers with what the company calls a “more aerodynamic shape,” and that phrase actually means something here. A bass port is just a tuned tube that lets low-frequency air pressure escape the cabinet in a controlled way. Shape that tube poorly and you get “chuffing,” a turbulent, slightly muddy rattle at high volumes. Shape it well and the air moves smoothly, which is why Marshall is calling the result cleaner vocals, wider sound dispersion, and more powerful low frequencies.

The tweeters got attention too. Reviewers who’ve actually heard the units describe upgraded tweeters and waveguides that aim to deliver a wider, more evenly dispersed and expansive soundstage. That solves a specific and annoying problem. Stand right in front of most bookshelf speakers and they sound great. Walk to the kitchen, and the vocals thin out. Marshall is trying to make the sound consistent no matter where you’re standing in the room.

There’s also a new feature called Dynamic Loudness. It automatically rebalances bass, mids, and treble depending on your volume level, so a quiet late-night listening session doesn’t sound thin and a loud party doesn’t distort. This isn’t a new idea. Every stereo receiver since the 1970s has had some version of a loudness button. Marshall is just doing it automatically, in software, without you touching a dial.

The Repairability Change Is the Bigger Story

Here’s what actually separates this launch from a typical yearly refresh. Marshall built the Acton IV and Stanmore IV with replaceable parts, specifically the front grille, control knobs, and rubber feet. If your speaker gets scratched, chewed by a dog, or just worn down after years on a shelf, you can now replace that one component instead of the entire $300–$400 unit.

This matters more than it sounds like on paper. Bluetooth speakers have historically been sealed, glued, single-use products. When the grille cracks or a knob wears smooth, the speaker isn’t broken, but it looks broken, and most people just buy a new one. Marshall building a parts ecosystem around a mainstream consumer speaker is a genuinely uncommon move, and it lines up with a broader push the company started with the Stockwell III earlier this year, which added a replaceable battery, strap, and grilles.

The cabinet itself is made from FSC 100%-certified wood, which is a sourcing certification confirming the MDF board came from responsibly managed forests. It won’t change how the speaker sounds. It will matter to anyone weighing the environmental cost of buying furniture-grade electronics every few years.

What Changed Physically, and Why the Power Cord Move Isn’t Trivial

Both speakers moved their power cord from the back to the bottom. That sounds like a footnote, but it solves a real placement problem. A speaker with a rear-mounted cable can’t sit flush against a wall, it needs an inch or two of clearance for the cord to bend, which either wastes shelf space or forces an awkward L-shaped cable run. With the power cable exiting from the bottom instead of the rear, both speakers can now sit flush against a wall. If you’ve ever tried to fit a bookshelf speaker into a tight gap between furniture, you know why this is worth mentioning.

The control layout got a refresh too, keeping Marshall’s signature brass panel but adding an upgraded media jog and a customizable M-button, which you can assign to a playlist, radio station, or EQ preset for one-press access. It’s the kind of feature you’ll either use constantly or never touch, depending on whether you have a fixed morning routine.

The Connectivity Upgrades You’ll Actually Notice

Both speakers now support Auracast, a Bluetooth LE Audio feature that lets you broadcast the same audio to multiple compatible speakers at once, even ones from other brands. If you’ve ever wanted two speakers playing in sync across a living room and kitchen without a proprietary multi-room ecosystem locking you in, this is the mechanism that makes it possible.

The Stanmore IV also keeps HDMI ARC, letting it double as a basic TV speaker, and both models gained an RCA input, which matters if you own a turntable. Vinyl output is a line-level analog signal, and RCA is still the standard connector for it, so this is a genuine convenience upgrade for anyone mixing streaming and physical media. LDAC support also carries over for higher-resolution Bluetooth streaming from compatible phones.

One notable absence: neither speaker adds Wi-Fi streaming. That’s a real limitation if you were hoping for the kind of always-on, higher-fidelity connection that Wi-Fi speakers offer over Bluetooth. Reviewers testing the Acton IV called this out directly as the one feature they wish it had, since Wi-Fi avoids the compression and range limits of Bluetooth entirely.

Who Should Actually Buy Which One

  • Get the Acton IV if you’re putting a speaker on a desk, bookshelf, or small-to-medium room. Its 4-inch woofer is sized for near-field listening, not filling a large open floor plan.
  • Get the Stanmore IV if you want one speaker to cover a living room or larger space. The 5-inch woofer gives it more low-end headroom before it starts to strain.
  • Skip both if your existing Acton III or Stanmore III still works and you don’t need Auracast or RCA input. The core sound signature hasn’t been reinvented, it’s been refined.

The Honest Bottom Line

Better bass ports and wider tweeters are the kind of upgrade every audio company claims every year, and skepticism about marketing language is fair. But the repairability push is different. It’s a structural decision, not a spec bump, and it’s the part of this launch that will still matter in three years when a knob finally wears out. Buy these speakers for the sound. Appreciate them for the fact that they’re built to last past the first scratch.
