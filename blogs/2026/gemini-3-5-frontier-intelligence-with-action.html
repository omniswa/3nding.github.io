Gemini 3.5: Frontier Intelligence With Action

May 20, 2026 9-Minute Read

Most AI announcements follow a familiar script: new benchmarks, impressive demos, carefully hedged comparisons. What Google showed at I/O 2026 was something different in character, if not in format. An AI agent built an operating system from scratch. Not a controlled demo environment, not a cherry-picked task—a full OS assembled by software subagents working in parallel, stitching components together autonomously. That demo was running on Gemini 3.5 Flash, and it signals a real inflection in what the company is building and what it wants this model family to be.

What Gemini 3.5 Actually Is

Gemini 3.5 is Google’s latest family of models combining frontier intelligence with action—a framing the company is using deliberately to separate it from prior generations that emphasized reasoning and comprehension above execution. [Google]

The first model in that family, Gemini 3.5 Flash, is now the default AI model in the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search worldwide. [Engadget] It replaced Gemini 3 Flash, itself a significant upgrade over 2.5 Flash, in the span of months—a pace that says something about how quickly Google is iterating.

The model is generally available as of May 19, 2026, with an API model ID of gemini-3.5-flash, priced at $1.50 per million input tokens and $9.00 per million output tokens, with a 1,048,576-token context window and dynamic thinking enabled by default. [LLM Leaderboard]

That pricing deserves a beat of attention. Google claims it often costs less than half the price of comparable frontier models [Google] , which matters enormously once you start running agents that make dozens or hundreds of API calls to complete a task. The economics of agentic AI are not like the economics of a single-turn chatbot query.

The Speed Argument Is Actually About Architecture

The headline number is that Gemini 3.5 Flash delivers 289 tokens per second, four times faster than other frontier models. [Glitchwire] That figure sounds like a raw performance stat, but the implications run deeper when you think about what agents actually do.

Agentic AI systems don’t wait for one call to finish before starting the next. Multiple subagents run in parallel, each making independent tool calls, generating intermediate outputs, and handing off context to other agents. In that environment, latency doesn’t add—it compounds. A model that responds in 250ms versus one that takes 2 seconds doesn’t save you 1.75 seconds per query; it saves you multiplicatively across hundreds of parallel operations.

Google co-developed 3.5 Flash alongside Antigravity, the company’s agentic development platform and IDE, precisely because the model was designed for that multi-agent environment from the start—not adapted to it afterward. [The Next Web] That co-development approach is a meaningful design choice. Most models are trained first and then deployed into agentic frameworks. Google built the framework and the model together.

An optimized version of Flash reportedly pushes the speed advantage to 12x faster than competing frontier models with equivalent quality [The Next Web] —a claim that hasn’t been independently verified at scale but is consistent with the benchmark data available.

What the Benchmarks Actually Show

On Terminal-Bench 2.1, Gemini 3.5 Flash scores 76.2%. On GDPval-AA, it reaches 1,656 Elo. On MCP Atlas for scaled tool use, it hits 83.6%. For multimodal understanding, it scores 84.2% on CharXiv Reasoning. [DataCamp]

These numbers need context to be meaningful. When Gemini 3.1 Pro launched, it scored 1,317 on GDPval-AA. Gemini 3.5 Flash scores 1,656—a step-change in agentic capability rather than incremental progress. [Glitchwire] That gap, achieved by a Flash-tier model in roughly three months, is the detail worth sitting with.

The more surprising claim is that 3.5 Flash outperforms Gemini 3.1 Pro on nearly all benchmarks, including coding, agentic tasks, and multimodal reasoning [The Next Web] —despite being a speed-optimized model positioned below the Pro tier in Google’s lineup. The traditional tradeoff between a “smart” model and a “fast” model has been at least partially broken here.

The important caveat: these are still Google’s own benchmarks, measured against Google’s own methodology. Claude Opus 4.7 leads GDPval-AA at 1,753 Elo [DataCamp] , which means 3.5 Flash is not at the absolute top of that particular leaderboard—it just beat Google’s own previous best. Independent third-party evaluation will tell a more complete story over the coming weeks.

The Antigravity Platform Matters as Much as the Model

There’s a tendency to focus on model releases as standalone events, but the more interesting story at I/O 2026 is the platform surrounding Gemini 3.5.

Google released Antigravity 2.0 alongside the model. The update turns the platform into a standalone desktop application built around agent-first development—a native environment where AI agents can live, work, and execute. It includes a CLI for terminal-first developers, an SDK for custom agent behaviors, and integrations with Google AI Studio, Firebase, and Android. [LLM Leaderboard]

A single API call can now spin up a full agent that reasons, uses tools, and executes code in an isolated Linux environment. Persistent environments resume across calls with files and state intact. [LLM Leaderboard]

This is Google’s answer to the fragmented agent tooling market, where developers have been stitching together LangChain, custom orchestration layers, and model API calls to build workflows that mostly work. Antigravity is a bet that the development environment itself becomes a competitive moat—that developers building on Google’s stack stay on Google’s stack because the tooling is native to the model.

Real Deployments, Concrete Use Cases

The demos were compelling. The enterprise traction is where things get verifiable.

Macquarie Bank is piloting 3.5 Flash to accelerate customer onboarding by reasoning over complex 100-plus-page documents, retrieving relevant information, and making recommendations with low latency. Salesforce is integrating it into Agentforce to automate enterprise tasks using multiple subagents that retain context and execute complex multi-turn tool calls. Xero is deploying agents to autonomously manage multi-week workflows, including identifying suppliers and gathering information for 1099 tax forms. Databricks is using agentic workflows to monitor real-time information and diagnose issues across massive datasets. [Google DeepMind]

Ramp is enabling smarter OCR through multimodal understanding of complex invoices combined with reasoning over historical patterns. [Google DeepMind]

These aren’t flashy demos. They’re tedious, high-volume, document-heavy enterprise workflows—exactly the kind of tasks that previously required either large teams or expensive specialized software. The fact that Gemini 3.5 Flash is handling them at scale, and at a lower per-token cost than competing models, is probably more significant for enterprise adoption than any benchmark number.

Gemini Spark: The Consumer-Facing Agent

Gemini Spark is Google’s new 24/7 personal AI agent, built on Gemini 3.5 Flash and integrated with Workspace apps including Gmail, Docs, and Slides, as well as more than 30 third-party integrations. It can continue operating in the background even after a laptop is closed or a phone is locked. [AndroidGuys]

Google says Spark operates under user control and asks for confirmation before taking higher-risk actions like sending emails or spending money. [AndroidGuys] That permission boundary design is worth noting—it’s Google’s public acknowledgment that “agentic” and “autonomous” can’t mean “unchecked.”

Gemini Spark is rolling out to trusted testers first, with a broader beta planned for U.S.-based Google AI Ultra subscribers in the following week. [Engadget]

A Counterpoint Worth Taking Seriously

The framing around Gemini 3.5 is almost entirely about capability. Understandably so—the numbers are good. But the broader consumer deployment raises real questions.

Google is currently facing a lawsuit after a man nearly committed a mass casualty event and died by suicide following weeks of chatting with Gemini. The implications for harm only grow when making powerful autonomous agents available more broadly. [TechCrunch]

Google says Gemini 3.5 has strengthened cyber and CBRN—chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear—safeguards and is better calibrated to engage with sensitive questions rather than refuse them outright. [TechCrunch] That’s a meaningful improvement over models that bluntly refuse edge-case queries, but it also represents a deliberate choice to expand the model’s willingness to engage—at exactly the moment it’s gaining the ability to act autonomously in the world.

The tension between capability and safety in agentic AI is not theoretical. It’s showing up in court filings and deployment decisions simultaneously.

What the Tier Compression Actually Signals

A Flash-tier model became a Pro-class benchmark result in roughly three months. If that compression continues, the distinction between flagship and efficient model tiers will keep collapsing. [Glitchwire]

This is the most structurally interesting observation from I/O 2026. Google has accelerated its release cadence dramatically. Gemini 3 Flash launched in December 2025. Gemini 3.1 Pro arrived in February 2026. Gemini 3.5 Flash shipped in May. Gemini 3.5 Pro is already in internal use and expected to roll out next month. [DataCamp]

At that pace, the question isn’t whether 3.5 Pro will be better than 3.5 Flash—it will be. The question is whether “better” at the Pro tier holds for long enough to matter before the next Flash model closes the gap again.

Tulsee Doshi, Google’s head of product for the Gemini model, framed the relationship between the two tiers directly: “3.5 Pro becomes your orchestrator, your planner, and then it can leverage Flash as the various subagents. It comes down to where you really want that reasoning power versus where you have tasks that merit good brute-force tool use capabilities.” [TechCrunch]

That’s a cleaner division of labor than the old “Pro for smart things, Flash for fast things” model. Pro reasons. Flash executes. In multi-agent architectures, both roles are critical, and you want the execution layer to be as fast and cheap as possible—which is exactly what 3.5 Flash is positioned to be.

Who Should Pay Attention Right Now

If you’re building production applications that involve document processing, code generation, multi-step research, or any workflow with repeated tool calls, the combination of 3.5 Flash’s speed, its pricing, and its 1M-token context window puts it in serious consideration immediately.

The model is available in the Gemini API, Google AI Studio, Google Antigravity, the Gemini app, and AI Mode in Google Search. [LLM Leaderboard] The tooling is there day one.

If you’re a developer evaluating AI platforms, the more consequential question is whether Antigravity’s native integration with the model gives it a real workflow advantage over third-party orchestration stacks—or whether it’s a walled garden that trades flexibility for convenience. That answer will come from building on it, not from benchmarks.

The Gemini app has grown from 400 million monthly users at last year’s I/O to more than 900 million users across 230 countries and 70 languages. [AndroidGuys] Whatever the technical merits, this model is about to be used by a lot of people—not as a research tool, but as the thing that runs in the background of their work and handles tasks they didn’t ask for manually. That’s a different kind of impact than any benchmark measures.
