Grok 4.5 vs Claude Opus: What "Opus-Class" Actually Means

July 9, 2026 7-Minute Read

Grok 4.5 does not beat Claude Opus. On every benchmark SpaceXAI published at launch, it scored lower. That’s the part most headlines skipped, and it’s also the most useful thing to know before you decide whether to switch anything over to it.

Here’s the short answer, since you’re probably here for it: Grok 4.5 is a coding and knowledge-work model released by SpaceXAI (formerly xAI) on July 8, 2026. Elon Musk called it “Opus-class,” meaning comparable in tier to Anthropic’s Claude Opus family, specifically Opus 4.7 rather than the newer Opus 4.8. It’s cheaper and faster than Opus. It is not, by the company’s own numbers, more capable.

The Quote That Started It

Musk posted the line himself on X: “It is an Opus-class model, but faster, more token-efficient and lower cost.” That’s a specific claim, and it’s worth taking apart piece by piece, because each part carries a different amount of weight.

“Opus-class” borrows Anthropic’s own tiering language. Anthropic uses Opus to mean its most capable, most expensive model family. So when Musk uses the same word, he’s not describing a benchmark score. He’s making a positioning claim, and positioning claims are cheap to make and expensive to verify.

He later clarified on X that Grok 4.5 is “roughly comparable to Opus 4.7, but much faster.” That’s a narrower claim than the first one. Opus 4.7 is Anthropic’s previous flagship. It’s already been replaced by Opus 4.8. So Grok 4.5 is being measured against last year’s top model, not this year’s.

Where the Benchmarks Actually Landed

Numbers settle arguments that adjectives can’t, so here’s what SpaceXAI published.

On DeepSWE 1.1, a benchmark that scores how reliably a model closes real developer-submitted bugs, Grok 4.5 hit 53%. Claude Opus 4.8 scored 59%. GPT-5.5 scored 67%. Claude Fable 5, Anthropic’s current top-tier model, topped the chart at 70%.

On SWE-Bench Pro, which scores resolution rate across a broader set of software engineering problems, Grok 4.5 posted 64.7%. Opus 4.8 came in at 69.2%. Fable 5 sat at 80.4%.

So the model that gets called “Opus-class” trails Opus on the exact benchmarks its own launch materials chose to show. That’s not a hidden detail. It’s printed right there in the chart. The interesting part isn’t that Grok lost. It’s that a company would publish results showing it losing and still frame the release around a comparison to the model that beat it.

Why “Losing” Might Still Be the Right Business Bet

This is where the story gets more interesting than a simple benchmark scoreboard, because raw capability isn’t the only thing that matters to a company paying for millions of tokens a month.

Grok 4.5 costs $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output tokens. Opus 4.8 costs $5 and $25 for the same. GPT-5.6 Luna, OpenAI’s model released the same week, prices at roughly $1 input and $6 output. So Grok isn’t the cheapest option on the table, but it undercuts Anthropic’s flagship by a wide margin, especially on output tokens, which is where costs usually pile up on long coding sessions.

Token efficiency compounds that gap further. On SWE-Bench Pro tasks, Grok 4.5 used an average of 15,954 output tokens to finish a job. Opus 4.8 used 67,020 tokens for comparable work, a 4.2x difference. Multiply that by thousands of daily runs, and the price-per-token comparison understates the real savings. A team running high-volume automated coding tasks might get through more iterations for less money on Grok, even while producing a lower-quality result per attempt.

Speed adds a third variable. Grok 4.5 runs at roughly 80 tokens per second, which sits in fast-model territory. For a developer waiting on an autocomplete suggestion or an agent looping through a multi-step task, speed changes how the tool feels to use, even when it doesn’t change what the tool can do.

Put those three together and Musk’s pitch stops sounding like spin. It’s a real tradeoff: lower capability for lower cost and higher speed. Whether that tradeoff makes sense depends entirely on what you’re building, which is a question no benchmark chart can answer for you.

The Cursor Connection Changes What This Model Is For

Grok 4.5 wasn’t built in isolation. It’s the first model trained jointly with Cursor, the AI coding platform SpaceX is in the process of acquiring in a deal reportedly valued at $60 billion. That matters because of where the training data came from.

SpaceXAI trained the model on developer session data from Cursor, meaning debugging traces and real code edits rather than static, scraped repositories. That’s a different kind of training signal than most foundation models get, and it explains why the launch pitch leans so heavily on coding, legal, and finance use cases rather than general chat. This isn’t being sold as a better conversationalist. It’s being sold as a better tool for people who spend their day inside a codebase or a contract.

The model itself is reportedly built on SpaceXAI’s V9 foundation model, a 1.5 trillion-parameter system, which is large by any standard, though parameter count on its own doesn’t predict benchmark performance the way it used to a few years back.

Where You Can Actually Use It Right Now

Grok 4.5 is live in Grok Build, in Cursor across all plans, and through the SpaceXAI console. Public rollout landed July 9, 2026, one day after the initial beta announcement. It is not yet available in the EU. SpaceXAI says European access is expected around mid-July, which is a pattern that’s become familiar with new frontier model launches given the additional regulatory review the EU AI Act requires.

If you’re outside the EU and want to try it, it’s already accessible today. If you’re inside the EU, the wait is measured in weeks, not months, based on the company’s own timeline.

The Compute Story Nobody’s Talking About

There’s a detail buried in this launch that’s stranger than the benchmark scores: SpaceXAI trained Grok 4.5 on the same compute capacity it leases out to Anthropic and Google. The training ran on tens of thousands of Nvidia GB300 GPUs inside Colossus, the Memphis-based supercomputer with total capacity across more than 200,000 GPUs.

That creates an odd dynamic going forward. As SpaceXAI’s own model training needs grow, it will eventually have to choose between reserving that capacity for its own models or renting it out to competitors as a revenue stream. Right now it’s doing both. That’s not sustainable indefinitely, and it’s worth watching which way that balance tips over the next year, because it says more about SpaceXAI’s actual strategy than any single model launch does.

What This Means If You’re Deciding What to Use

None of this means Grok 4.5 is a bad model. It means the “Opus-class” label describes positioning, not benchmark supremacy, and the two get conflated constantly in AI marketing because most readers never check the fine print on the chart.

If your priority is squeezing maximum capability out of a coding agent regardless of cost, the published numbers say Opus 4.8 and Claude Fable 5 currently sit ahead of Grok 4.5. If your priority is running high volume at a lower bill, with speed that keeps a coding workflow feeling responsive, Grok 4.5’s price and token efficiency make a real case for itself, benchmarks aside.

The launch also landed on a genuinely crowded day. GPT-5.6 rolled out publicly the same week, following a staggered release the Trump administration reportedly requested. Three frontier-adjacent releases inside a few days is unusual even by this industry’s pace, and it’s a reminder that whatever model looks best this week is a fast-moving target, not a settled ranking.

Check current pricing and benchmark pages directly before making a purchasing decision, since both numbers move often in this market and today’s comparison chart won’t stay accurate for long.
