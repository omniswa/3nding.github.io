Nvidia CEO: You're Losing Your Job to Bad Managers, Not AI

May 27, 2026 6-Minute Read

On May 26, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang sat for an interview with Singapore broadcaster CNA and said something tech executives almost never say: the narrative connecting AI to job losses is “just too lazy.” [Fast Company] He wasn’t being diplomatic. He called it “a way for them to sound smart” and said plainly, “I really hate that.” [Fast Company]

By that point, the numbers had become grim. As of mid-May 2026, over 113,000 tech workers had lost their jobs across 179 companies—an average of 825 people per day since January 1. [Tech Journal] That figure had already crossed 142,000 by late May. [Tech Research Online] The companies doing the cutting framed it as inevitable. Microsoft cited AI when it cut 15,000 people. Amazon blamed AI when it eliminated 16,000 roles. Oracle eliminated 30,000 roles globally. [M9.news] The pattern was ritualistic: announce layoffs, invoke AI, move on.

Huang’s objection wasn’t that AI wouldn’t displace workers eventually. His point was simpler and more damning: “AI has just arrived. How is it possible they’re already losing jobs? How is it possible that AI became productive and useful only six months ago, and they were somehow laying people off two years ago because of AI?” [AOL]

The logical gap he identified is real. Most of the AI tools companies cite today—GPT-4, Claude, specialized coding assistants—reached actual workplace deployment within the last 18 months. Yet the companies eliminating headcount did so well before these tools existed or proved measurable value. The timeline doesn’t fit. Either companies were cutting for reasons unrelated to AI and borrowed the narrative for cover, or they’re claiming causation where none exists.

This matters because the explanation we accept shapes how workers respond and how leaders act next.

The Real Pattern Underlying the Cuts

The picture becomes clearer when you separate AI from everything else happening simultaneously. Tech companies are firing people while spending more money than ever: Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, and Alphabet collectively committed roughly $725 billion in capital expenditure in 2026, a 75% increase over 2025, almost entirely earmarked for AI data centers, chips, and infrastructure. [Tech Journal] That’s not layoff-driven belt-tightening. That’s capital reallocation. They’re freeing cash from payroll to fund infrastructure spending.

Confirming this requires looking at when companies actually blame AI versus other factors. According to Challenger, Gray & Christmas data, AI is the fifth most common reason for job cuts, trailing market conditions, restructuring, closures, and cost-cutting. [Tech Journal] When hiring cycles collapse and interest rates rise, companies restructure. When a new technology arrives, they retrospectively explain the restructuring through it. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said, “There’s some AI washing where people are blaming AI for layoffs that they would otherwise do.” [Tom’s Hardware] Venture capitalist Marc Andreessen attributed layoffs primarily to pandemic-era overhiring and higher interest rates, adding that companies now have “the silver bullet excuse: ‘Ah, it’s AI.’” [Tech Journal]

The evidence hints at this too. MIT’s 2025 State of AI in Business found that firms invested an estimated $30–40 billion in generative AI, yet 95 percent of the AI pilots studied never moved beyond the testing phase. [house] If 95% of AI projects don’t scale past prototype, the productivity gains companies claim when announcing layoffs likely don’t exist yet.

This creates a peculiar situation: companies are cutting people based on AI productivity gains that haven’t materialized at scale.

What Huang Actually Told Workers

Huang’s message to people worried about losing their jobs was direct and uncommon for a tech CEO: “You’re not losing your job to AI, but to someone who uses AI better.” [AOL] The distinction matters. AI isn’t the threat; incompetence is. Workers who treat these tools as novelties or ignore them will be less productive than colleagues who integrate them into daily work. Productivity gaps compound. Over time, organizations will keep the people moving faster and cut the people moving slower.

This isn’t new. Huang compared the current moment to the arrival of the personal computer, which did not eliminate work but changed which workers stayed competitive. Those who adapted thrived. Those who did not were left behind. [AOL] Excel didn’t eliminate financial analysis roles; it eliminated financial analysts who didn’t learn Excel. The medium changed. The work persisted for people who adapted.

He also offered workers a longer-term perspective. Huang said it is “very likely” there will be more jobs in five years than there are today. [AOL] This claim sits awkwardly alongside Boston Consulting Group’s projection that up to 15% of U.S. jobs could be eliminated over the next five years [Tech Journal] —a number that includes all sectors, not just tech. But Huang’s framing assumes creation-on-top: jobs disappear in some categories, new jobs emerge in others, and the net shifts toward the sectors investing in new technology. That’s historically how industrial transitions work, though the assertion outpaces the evidence we have today.

The Uncomfortable Middle Ground

The honest take is that neither the catastrophists nor the optimists have fully captured what’s happening in 2026. About 44% of CFOs at U.S. companies say they plan on some AI-related job cuts in 2026, with about 502,000 roles expected to be cut by year-end. [Fortune] That’s substantial—a real shock to real people. Simultaneously, some companies buck the layoff trend. IBM has reportedly tripled its entry-level hiring in 2026, saying that while AI can do many entry-level jobs, it still needs a human touch. [Tom’s Hardware] Different companies are making different bets about where human labor still matters.

The severance narratives also reveal something. When Atlassian announced 1,600 layoffs representing 10% of its workforce, CEO Mike Cannon-Brookes said: “It would be disingenious to pretend AI doesn’t change the mix of skills we need or the number of roles required in certain areas.” [Tech Insider] He didn’t claim total displacement. He acknowledged skill-mix shift—the capability gap between someone who can use AI fluently and someone who can’t. This is narrower and more specific than “AI will eliminate your job.” It says: the job stays, but the person in it needs to change.

Huang’s critique, then, isn’t that AI has zero impact on employment. It’s that companies are conflating workforce restructuring with technological inevitability. They’re cutting people, calling it AI, and moving capital to infrastructure without waiting to know whether the infrastructure investment will actually pay off. The moral hazard is obvious: why bother optimizing the work you have if you can shrink the team instead?

The harder question is whether workers should take Huang’s optimism at face value. Huang runs Nvidia, which benefits enormously from companies betting on AI regardless of whether those bets succeed. His interest in maintaining optimism is not neutral. But he’s also pointing at a real logical inconsistency in how companies explain their decisions.

What workers actually need is clarity: Am I being cut because a technology has proven it can do my job better, or because my company miscalculated headcount two years ago and needs a socially acceptable story for reductions? The answer determines whether your next move is “learn AI tools” or “find a company that didn’t over-hire.”

Huang thinks the distinction matters. So far, few other executives have bothered to acknowledge it even exists.
