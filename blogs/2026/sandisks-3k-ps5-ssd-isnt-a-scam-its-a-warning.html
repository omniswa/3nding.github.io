SanDisk's $3K PS5 SSD Isn't a Scam. It's a Warning.

June 19, 2026 7-Minute Read

A $3,000 SSD for a $649 console sounds like satire. It isn’t.

On June 16, 2026, SanDisk announced the Optimus GX PRO 850P NVMe SSD, an officially licensed storage drive for the PlayStation 5 and PS5 Pro. The lineup starts at $380 for 1TB, climbs to $760 for 2TB, and reaches $1,500 for 4TB. The 8TB flagship—listed at a regular price of $3,700, currently “discounted” to $2,959.99—is, by a significant margin, the most expensive consumer SSD ever marketed to mainstream gamers.

That $2,960 figure is 3.3 times the price of the PS5 Pro and more than 4.5 times the cost of the base PS5. [Tech4Gamers] The internet reacted with predictable fury. But fury misses the actual story. The price is outrageous. It is also entirely explicable. And the explanation has almost nothing to do with SanDisk.

The Drive Itself Isn’t the Problem

The Optimus GX PRO isn’t vaporware. It delivers PCIe 4.0 read and write speeds of up to 7,300 MB/s and 6,600 MB/s respectively, comes with an integrated heatsink bearing the PlayStation logo, and carries a 5-year warranty. [TweakTown] Its specifications closely resemble those of the WD_BLACK SN850X—a popular PlayStation-compatible drive that SanDisk appears to be replacing following its branding transition away from the WD_BLACK name. [Dexerto]

The SN850X was, until recently, one of the most recommended PS5 storage expansions on the market. Reliable. Fast. Reasonably priced.

Then the world changed.

What’s Actually Driving This Price

The global memory chip shortage of 2026 has become one of the most consequential supply chain crises in the technology industry’s recent history. Data centers now consume an estimated 70% of all memory chips produced worldwide, and consumers are bearing the brunt of a structural shift that analysts say will persist well into 2027. [Tech Insider]

Here’s the mechanism. Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron control over 95% of global DRAM and NAND flash production. They are making a rational business decision: they have systematically reallocated manufacturing capacity toward high-bandwidth memory (HBM) chips used in AI accelerators, leaving consumer-grade DRAM and NAND flash in critically short supply. [Tech Insider]

HBM is the memory that powers AI chips—Nvidia H100s, B200s, the hardware running every large language model inference at scale. It commands premium prices. It comes with long-term purchase agreements from hyperscalers like Meta, Google, and Microsoft. OpenAI’s “Stargate” project alone has signed agreements with Samsung and SK Hynix for up to 900,000 wafers of DRAM per month—a figure that could account for close to 40% of global DRAM output. [Tom’s Hardware]

When a single AI infrastructure deal can absorb 40% of the world’s DRAM, there is simply less left for everything else.

How Bad the Numbers Actually Are

Kingston’s Datacenter SSD Business Manager reported a 246% increase in NAND wafer pricing compared to Q1 2025. Samsung raised contract prices between 30% and 60% versus September 2025 levels. TrendForce projected NAND flash prices to increase 33-38% quarter-over-quarter in Q1 2026 alone. [NAND Research]

Consumer SSDs felt this immediately. A 1TB consumer SSD that cost approximately $45 in late 2025 now costs close to $90. [NAND Research] That’s a near-doubling in months, not years.

For larger drives the math gets uglier faster. As noted by PC Part Picker, a near-identical 8TB SSD from SanDisk would have cost around $640 less than a year ago—representing a 370% increase in price. [Videogames Chronicle]

370% in one year. That number needs to sit still for a moment.

Capacity expansion meaningful enough to ease pricing is unlikely until late 2027 or 2028. [TrendForce] Kioxia, one of the top NAND manufacturers, has publicly stated that its entire NAND flash production for 2026 is already sold out. [RedShark News] You cannot buy what isn’t being made for you.

Gaming Is Paying the Bill for the AI Boom

The SanDisk drive is the most dramatic example, but it isn’t an isolated one.

In March 2026, Sony raised PS5 prices by at least $100 across all models—its biggest single increase yet—citing ongoing global manufacturing cost increases. Since Sony’s last price revision, DRAM contract prices reportedly increased by over 170% year-over-year. [Videogames Chronicle] The base PS5 now costs $649.99. The PS5 Pro runs $899.99.

Microsoft raised Xbox Series X prices twice in the previous year. A standard Xbox Series X now retails for $649.99, up $150 from launch. [Videogames Chronicle]

Valve’s Steam Deck OLED, long praised as the value champion of handheld PCs, saw its pricing jump in 2026: the 512GB model moved from $549 to $789, and the 1TB OLED model went from $649 to $949. [Tech Insider]

This is not a coincidence of greedy companies. Every piece of gaming hardware contains memory. Memory is in shortage. The price flows downstream into everything downstream of it.

Who This Drive Is Actually For

Nobody buying a $2,960 SSD for their PS5 is confused about what they’re doing. This is not a product for the median gamer.

SanDisk claims the 8TB model can store up to 200 PS5 games based on average installation sizes. [Ground News] That’s a meaningful number for someone who plays constantly, maintains large game libraries, and simply refuses to manage storage manually. Content creators capturing gameplay in high resolution also need fast, high-capacity drives. The pricing doesn’t ease up much for more modest capacities either—the 2TB model retails for over $100 more than a base PS5 console. [eTeknix]

The honest reality is that SanDisk has launched this drive now because they had to—if they want an official PS5 product in the market at all, these are the costs. The alternative is selling nothing.

What Relief Looks Like—and When

IDC projects 2026 DRAM supply growth of only 16% year-on-year, with NAND supply growth at just 17%—both figures sitting well below the 20-30% historical norms that defined the post-2018 memory market. [Storageswiss]

TrendForce projects the total memory market to reach $551.6 billion in 2026 and surge to $842.7 billion in 2027. NAND flash market revenue alone is expected to grow 112% year-over-year in 2026. Demand is not cooling. It is accelerating. [Storageswiss]

The driver is generative AI moving from training to inference. More models. More queries. More memory required per query, every day.

The consensus among industry analysts is that meaningful relief won’t arrive until late 2027 at the earliest, and virtually all new capacity is earmarked for HBM and AI memory. [IQON Digital]

So if you’re waiting for the $100 1TB SSD to come back, you’re waiting at least 18 months—probably longer.

The Practical Takeaway for PS5 Owners

Don’t buy the 8TB Optimus GX PRO. Almost certainly. The value proposition collapses immediately when you compare it to managing your library more carefully or deleting and reinstalling games as needed. That is free. Three thousand dollars is not.

For storage expansion that actually makes sense right now: the 1TB tier of any PS5-compatible PCIe 4.0 drive still offers the best cost-per-gigabyte ratio, even at elevated prices. The jump from 1TB to 2TB rarely scales proportionally in value. Buy the minimum that resolves your immediate problem, and revisit in late 2027 when there is at least a chance the market has loosened.

The SanDisk Optimus GX PRO 8TB is a real product for a real problem—running out of fast, reliable storage on a console that increasingly hosts 100GB+ game installs. The price is real too, and it comes from a real supply crisis that has nothing to do with PlayStation and everything to do with who controls the global memory supply chain and what they’ve decided to do with it.

AI built the shortage. Gamers are paying for it.
