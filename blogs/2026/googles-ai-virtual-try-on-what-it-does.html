Google's AI Virtual Try-On: What It Does

June 4, 2026 10-Minute Read

Online clothing returns cost U.S. retailers an estimated $890 billion in 2024. The problem isn’t sizing charts—shoppers have had those for decades. The problem is that a flat product photo on a white background tells you almost nothing about how a piece of fabric will actually behave on your specific body.

Google decided to attack that problem at scale.

This Isn’t the Virtual Try-On You Ignored in 2019

There’s an important distinction worth making upfront. The version of “virtual try-on” that flopped years ago was mostly AR gimmickry—a clothing overlay that looked like it was pasted on with masking tape. What Google has built is architecturally different.

The current system is powered by a custom image generation model built specifically for fashion. It understands the human body and the physical behavior of fabric—how different materials fold, stretch, and drape across different body types. These subtleties are preserved even when applied to varying poses in user-uploaded photos. [Google] That last part is the hard part. Anyone can slap a shirt graphic onto a torso. Simulating the physics of a linen blend on a real human in motion is a different problem entirely.

Google’s system uses a diffusion-based image generation model trained on pairs of photos showing people in different poses wearing the same garment. From that, the AI learns how fabric drapes, casts shadows, and folds across different human body types—and with just one product image, it can generate a realistic render of that item on a person. [SEO Sherpa]

This matters because it means brands don’t have to do anything special. No expensive photoshoots. No 3D scans of every SKU. The AI does the visual physics from a single reference image.

The Scale Is the Story

The technology itself is interesting. The scale is what makes it genuinely disruptive.

Google’s Shopping Graph is a real-time dataset of over 50 billion product listings—from major global retailers to local shops—each with details like reviews, prices, color options, and availability. Every hour, more than 2 billion of those listings are refreshed. [Google] [Digital Commerce 360]

Plugging a working virtual try-on model into that infrastructure means the feature is effectively available on almost everything you can find in Google Shopping. Not just a curated selection. Not just partner brands. Billions of items.

For context, by mid-2024, the AI research community had gotten latent diffusion models to produce high-fidelity fabric rendering—but at the cost of heavy compute and slow inference. Google’s own TPUs and data center infrastructure allowed them to push inference speed to a place where this is viable in a consumer product at scale. [Medium] The technical problem had been solved in labs. Google industrialized it.

How to Actually Use It Right Now

The feature has evolved fast since its initial rollout in 2023, and the current version is meaningfully different from what launched.

Originally, users had to upload a full-body photo. As of December 2025, you can use a selfie. Google uses Nano Banana—its Gemini 2.5 Flash Image model—to generate a full-body digital version of you from just a face photo. You select your usual size, and the system generates several candidate images. You pick one as your default try-on photo. [TechCrunch]

If you want to use the full feature set:

  1. Opt into the “Try On” experiment via Google Search Labs (U.S. only, currently)
  2. Search for any clothing item on Google Shopping
  3. Tap the “try it on” icon on any product listing
  4. Upload a full-body photo—or now, just a selfie
  5. The AI generates multiple versions of you wearing the item

As of October 2025, the feature expanded beyond clothing to shoes, and beyond the U.S. to Australia, Canada, and Japan. To try on shoes, you tap any footwear listing, select “Try It On,” and add a full-length photo. The result appears in seconds. [TechCrunch]

For best results, Google’s own guidance is specific: good lighting, fitted clothing in the uploaded photo, full-body framing. Loose clothing in your reference photo gives the model ambiguous body information to work with, and output quality degrades accordingly.

The Doppl App Is the More Ambitious Bet

Embedded inside Google Search is useful. But Google Labs launched a separate, dedicated app in June 2025 called Doppl, and it reveals where this is actually heading.

Doppl builds on the Shopping feature but adds one significant capability: it turns static try-on images into AI-generated videos. The dynamic visuals are designed to give users a better sense of how an outfit would actually feel and move—not just how it looks pinned flat on a static avatar. [PYMNTS.com]

Users upload a full-body photo or screenshot of any outfit from anywhere—a friend’s social post, an influencer’s photo, a Pinterest pin—and Doppl generates a short video showing how those clothes might look and move on them. Unlike the Shopping integration, which is product-listing-specific, Doppl accepts inspiration from virtually any visual source. [Salt]

That’s a meaningful unlock. It shifts the tool from “shopping utility” to something closer to a personal style sandbox.

As of late 2025, Google updated Doppl with a shoppable discovery feed. The app generates personalized style recommendations based on user preferences, shows AI-generated video of real products, and links directly to merchants for purchase. [Techbuzz] The architecture of that feature is a direct challenge to TikTok Shop’s discovery model—except the try-on is built in.

All outputs from Doppl carry an invisible SynthID watermark, Google’s tool for identifying AI-generated media. It doesn’t affect image quality, but it provides content traceability—relevant both for platform trust and for the inevitable questions about deepfake-adjacent misuse. [DaveAI]

What the Data Actually Says About Impact

The skeptic’s question is reasonable: does any of this change purchasing behavior, or is it just a novelty?

According to a September 2024 eMarketer report, virtual try-on images in Google Search receive 60% more high-quality views than standard product listings. On average, shoppers try on clothing using four different models per product, and they’re measurably more likely to visit a brand’s website after engaging with the tool. [Digital Commerce 360]

That last metric is the one that matters to retailers. Traffic intent signals from someone who just tried on your jacket is qualitatively different from someone who glanced at a thumbnail.

Estée Lauder saw a 2.5x lift in conversions after launching a similar AI-powered try-on tool with Perfect Corp. e.l.f. Cosmetics introduced a photo-based try-on for more than 320 products and reported a 200% increase in conversions. [Digital Commerce 360] These are cosmetics, not apparel—different category dynamics—but the directional evidence is consistent.

Market research firm Valuates projects the global virtual try-on market will grow from $5.8 billion in 2024 to $27.7 billion by 2031, driven by smartphone adoption, AI personalization, and high return rates in fashion and cosmetics. [Digital Commerce 360] The return rate problem alone justifies the investment. Returns are expensive. They’re logistically messy, environmentally costly, and they represent a direct drag on retail margin.

Where It Still Falls Short

Be honest about this: the technology is impressive, and it is also imperfect.

In testing, the AI sometimes generates avatars that are slimmer or more distorted than the actual user. Animation can fail to load. The more complex the garment—ruffles, sheer fabrics, layered textures—the more likely the output is to show artifacts or misrepresent fit. [Salt]

Dresses are a case study in that problem. Google acknowledged in 2024 that dresses are technically harder than tops and blouses because they’re more detailed and complex. To address this, Google developed a new technique called the VTO-UNet Diffusion Transformer (VTO-UDiT), designed to preserve the person’s features while more accurately erasing and replacing the dress in the generated image. [yahoo]

The engineering response to that problem is the right one. But it illustrates that “billions of clothing items” doesn’t mean “billions of clothing items rendered with equal accuracy.” Category complexity still matters.

There’s also the body image question, which serious observers aren’t dismissing. The AI-generated images, however sophisticated, may not always accurately represent the user’s true body dimensions. The concern is that systematic distortion—even unintentional—creates unrealistic expectations. [OpenTools.ai] Google hasn’t published anything specific on how it audits for that kind of output bias. That’s a gap.

What This Means If You’re in Retail

If you’re a brand or retailer, the practical implication is straightforward: your product listings in Google Shopping now have try-on functionality by default, without any action required on your end. The model works from a single product image. You do not need 3D models, custom integrations, or AR assets.

The flip side is that you don’t control the output. If a garment is cut in a way that renders badly under the current model’s assumptions, a shopper will generate an unflattering image and potentially walk away from a purchase they would have made based on a well-shot product photo.

The longer-term implication is about search intent and ad economics. Google’s Chief Business Officer Philipp Schindler noted on the company’s Q2 earnings call that people can now try billions of clothing products on themselves virtually. [Digital Commerce 360] Google doesn’t surface a feature at that scale accidentally. The engagement data flowing from try-on interactions is purchase intent data—and purchase intent data is what Google’s advertising business runs on. The feature is genuinely useful for shoppers. It’s also an extraordinarily efficient intent-capture mechanism for a company that sells ads against commercial searches.

That’s not cynicism. It’s just the underlying economics of why this feature exists at Google’s scale and not at a startup’s.

The Competitive Pressure This Creates

Amazon has virtual try-on for footwear and accessories. Walmart has it for apparel. Neither operates at the scale of Google’s Shopping Graph, and neither has a standalone discovery app attempting to compete with TikTok’s shopping feed simultaneously.

For cosmetics and hair, Google relies on AR overlays powered by partners like Perfect Corp and PulpoAR [SEO Sherpa] , which signals that even Google isn’t trying to own every vertical of this technology outright. Strategic partnerships fill the gaps the diffusion model doesn’t handle well.

The trajectory from here is predictable: more categories, more countries, higher fidelity on edge cases, and eventually agentic checkout built on top of try-on data. Google has already previewed an agentic checkout feature designed to help users act quickly when the price is right, layered on top of the same Shopping Graph infrastructure. [Google]

The virtual dressing room is table stakes. What comes after it—an AI that knows your taste, has seen your body, and can execute a purchase in your price range without you touching a checkout page—is the actual product being built.
