Nokia Shares Jump on Cisco's AI Network Surge

May 18, 2026 9-Minute Read

When a competitor’s earnings report lifts your stock by double digits, the market is telling you something specific. Not that you’re riding coattails — but that the thesis has been confirmed at scale.

That’s what happened on May 13, 2026. Cisco reported quarterly revenue of $15.84 billion, up 12% year-over-year, while networking revenue jumped 25% to $8.82 billion — ahead of analyst estimates of $8.47 billion. [Investing.com] The report sent Cisco shares soaring more than 18% in premarket trading. And Nokia shares jumped more than 10% in the same session — not because Nokia had reported anything, but because Cisco’s numbers validated exactly the infrastructure spending trend Nokia has been quietly positioning for.

What Cisco’s Numbers Actually Confirmed

Cisco said it has received $5.3 billion in AI infrastructure and hyperscaler orders so far this fiscal year and raised its full-year AI order forecast to $9 billion from $5 billion. It also lifted its revenue outlook for that AI market to $4 billion, up from a prior estimate of $3 billion. [Investing.com]

Those aren’t incremental revisions. Doubling a full-year forecast mid-year is a signal that demand is arriving faster than anyone modeled. And the category driving that demand — high-speed network infrastructure for AI data centers — is the same space Nokia has been methodically entering through optical networking and IP routing gear.

The results reflect growing corporate spending on high-speed network infrastructure needed to support AI data centers. [Investing.com] That’s the clean summary, but the underlying dynamic is more structural: every major cloud provider is now building or expanding AI training clusters that require optical interconnects between thousands of GPUs across multiple racks and buildings. Cisco sells the switching and routing equipment. Nokia sells the fiber and optical systems that carry the traffic between facilities and across continents. The two companies are not competing here — they occupy different but adjacent layers of the same buildout.

Why Nokia Specifically Benefited

Nokia’s sympathy move wasn’t sector-wide noise. The stock has been undergoing a genuine repositioning, and Cisco’s results gave that story a hard data point to anchor against.

In the first quarter of 2026, Nokia’s AI and cloud-related net sales surged 49% year over year, contributing to €1 billion in new orders. The company also raised its forecast for AI and cloud growth from 16% to a 27% compound annual growth rate through 2028, reflecting hyperscaler capital expenditure acceleration. [The Motley Fool]

That 49% growth figure is the number that actually matters for investors trying to assess whether Nokia’s AI exposure is real or aspirational. It’s real. Nokia’s Q1 2026 results showed EPS of €0.05, up from €0.03 the prior year, on revenue of €4.5 billion — a 4% increase — powered by AI and Cloud and Network Infrastructure strength. [StocksToTrade] The topline beat was modest. The margin expansion and order quality were not.

Traders care less about headline beats and more about where the growth engine is. For Nokia, that engine is traffic from hyperscalers and AI workloads, not legacy base stations. [Timothy Sykes]

Nokia’s Restructuring Is Actually the Story

Much of the attention lands on the stock move itself. The more interesting development is what Nokia did internally to make itself relevant to this spending cycle.

Nokia reorganized its portfolio effective January 1, 2026, designating Network Infrastructure as a growth segment positioned to capitalize on the rapid, global AI and data center buildout. The segment consolidates Optical Networks, IP Networks, and Fixed Networks under a single structure led by David Heard. [sec]

CEO Justin Hotard, who took over in 2024, has been explicit about de-emphasizing the parts of the business that don’t benefit from AI capex and doubling down on optical and IP routing. The acquisition of Infinera — completed in late 2024 — gave Nokia a significantly stronger optical networking portfolio, particularly for hyperscale data center interconnects. Nokia carries trailing twelve-month revenue of roughly $20 billion across Network Infrastructure, Mobile Infrastructure, and Nokia Technologies. CEO Justin Hotard has reorganized the portfolio and absorbed Infinera to sharpen the optical story. [24/7 Wall St.]

The Infinera deal is worth understanding specifically: Infinera’s coherent optical technology sits at the intersection of long-haul backbone networks and the high-capacity links hyperscalers use between data centers. That’s ground zero for the traffic explosion that AI inference workloads are creating. Nokia paid for that position; now it’s starting to monetize it.

The Valuation Question

Here’s the honest tension. NOK is up roughly 116% since the year started and has gained about 169% over the past 52 weeks, closing at $13.98 on May 15. But the valuation has expanded accordingly — the stock now trades at 33.72x trailing earnings and 27.59x cash flow, both above sector medians of 24.52x and 18.01x. [FinancialContent]

For a company that spent years being dismissed as a legacy telecom supplier, paying above-median multiples requires the growth story to hold. Two things support it: the order book quality and the earnings trajectory. In Q4 2025, Nokia’s Optical Networks posted 17% growth, with book-to-bill remaining above one, driven by demand from AI and Cloud customers. [sec] A book-to-bill above one means orders are coming in faster than the company is converting them to revenue — backlog is building, not shrinking.

The counterpoint: Nokia’s Mobile Networks business, which includes 5G radio and core equipment sold to traditional carriers, remains under pressure. Carrier capex for 5G has been uneven globally, and Nokia is not immune to that softness. Argus notes that Nokia’s Mobile Networks environment has been stable but could begin to grow as carriers expand capacity to support AI data center traffic — a stabilization that, after years of decline in the 5G capex cycle, could turn Nokia from a value trap into a credible growth-and-income story. [24/7 Wall St.]

That framing — value trap evolving into growth-and-income — captures the risk symmetry well. If the AI infrastructure buildout sustains, Nokia’s optical and IP networking segments can grow fast enough to more than offset mobile weakness. If hyperscaler capex cools or concentrates in ways that favor fewer vendors, Nokia’s premium multiple will look harder to justify.

What the Cisco Comparison Reveals

Cisco and Nokia are often discussed as if they’re direct competitors. They’re not, and the distinction matters for understanding the Nokia investment case.

Cisco dominates enterprise networking — campus switches, enterprise routers, security appliances — alongside its growing position in hyperscale switching. Nokia’s primary networking revenue comes from service providers (carriers, telcos) and increasingly from hyperscale optical interconnect. The two companies share customers at the top of the hyperscaler tier, but they’re generally selling different layers of the stack.

What Cisco’s results revealed is that enterprise and hyperscale customers are opening their wallets broadly for network upgrades tied to AI workloads. AI buildouts are picking up speed, with major cloud companies planning to spend hundreds of billions in 2026 to handle larger training clusters and inference workloads. [Yahoo Finance] That spending benefits both companies, but through different product lines and customer segments. Nokia’s leverage is more concentrated — it depends more heavily on a smaller number of hyperscale accounts for its optical growth, while Cisco’s AI revenue is spread across more customers. That concentration is both Nokia’s opportunity and its risk factor.

Beyond Data Centers: Defense and Broadband

Nokia has also been making moves that don’t show up cleanly in the AI infrastructure narrative but matter for diversification.

Nokia Federal Solutions and Lockheed Martin teamed up to introduce a mission-critical 5G solution for the U.S. Department of Defense, using open architecture standards. This modular 5G setup brings Nokia’s carrier-grade 5G into the DoD’s framework, so military vehicles and platforms can tap into commercial 5G while in the field. [Yahoo Finance]

Defense contracts are sticky, high-margin, and insulated from the commercial spending cycles that can make telecom equipment revenue lumpy. Getting into the DoD’s 5G architecture at a foundational level — particularly through open standards that favor vendor diversification — positions Nokia for follow-on contracts that could extend for a decade.

In May 2026, Nokia also rolled out AI tools for home and broadband networks, drawing on experience from more than 600 million broadband lines it’s deployed globally. The telecom industry is expected to pour $6.2 billion into this kind of AI by 2030. [Yahoo Finance]

What Comes Next

Nokia’s next earnings report is set for July 23. Analysts are expecting $0.07 per share for the June quarter, up from $0.05 a year earlier — a 40% jump year-over-year. Wall Street is starting to treat Nokia as a real AI and networking play. [FinancialContent]

The July report will matter more than usual. The Q1 results established the trend. Q2 will either confirm that Nokia’s AI and Cloud growth rate is durable or reveal whether the 49% surge was partially timing-driven — pull-forward from hyperscalers placing large orders ahead of infrastructure deadlines. Order intake trajectory and whether book-to-bill stays above one will be the numbers to watch, not just the headline revenue figure.

Nokia raised its growth targets for its AI business, now projecting the addressable market for AI and cloud to expand 27% annually between 2025 and 2028, up from a prior estimate of 16%. [Investing.com] That upward revision to the addressable market estimate is the company’s own signal that its original assumptions were too conservative. When a management team raises the addressable market forecast mid-cycle, they’re telling you the pipeline is stronger than the original model assumed.

The Cisco report confirmed the spend is real. The Nokia reorganization targeted the right segment. The Infinera acquisition bought the optical capability to compete for it. Whether the stock has priced all of that in already — or whether the run from $9 to nearly $14 still leaves room — depends entirely on how durable the hyperscaler capex cycle turns out to be. That’s a macro call. The company’s execution, at least for now, is not the variable in question.
