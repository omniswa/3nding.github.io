MediaTek's Dual Packaging Bet: EMIB vs CoWoS

May 15, 2026 9-Minute Read

When Google’s next-generation TPU design crossed a threshold TSMC said didn’t exist—chips too large for any CoWoS variant in production—something had to give. That something turned out to be the assumption that the AI chip supply chain ran through Taiwan and only Taiwan. MediaTek, the company designing those TPUs, is now reportedly running a dual advanced packaging strategy: TSMC CoWoS for current production, Intel EMIB for the chips that physically cannot fit inside CoWoS’s limits. The split isn’t a hedge. It’s an engineering constraint dressed up as strategy.

Why Packaging Became the Constraint

The semiconductor conversation spent years fixated on node shrinks—3nm, 2nm, the race to angstrom-era transistors. Packaging was the unglamorous back-end problem, the thing you solved after the silicon was done. That framing aged poorly.

Today, advanced packaging is the bottleneck that determines whether a chip ships at all. TSMC’s CoWoS—Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate—became the default platform for every serious AI accelerator after NVIDIA chose it for the H100. The H200, GB200, and AMD’s MI300 all run on CoWoS variants. The technology works by mounting dies onto a silicon interposer, which then sits on a substrate. It delivers high bandwidth and tight die-to-die interconnect density. It’s also capacity-constrained, size-limited, and controlled by exactly one company.

TSMC is scaling CoWoS capacity from around 35,000 wafers per month in late 2024 to 130,000 by end of 2026—an 80% compound annual growth rate—but demand from hyperscalers still outpaces that expansion. When you are building AI infrastructure at Google’s scale and your packaging supplier has a waiting list, you start looking at alternatives.

The Size Problem No One Planned For

The immediate trigger for MediaTek’s packaging split is geometric. Google’s next-generation TPU, codenamed “HumuFish,” has a silicon interposer that has reportedly reached nine times the reticle size—nearly 80 square centimeters—almost three times larger than the previous generation and approaching the hard limit of TSMC’s CoWoS technology.

TSMC’s CoWoS packaging limit sits at 9.5 reticles, roughly 120 square centimeters. “TSMC currently cannot support chips of that size,” according to one foreign analyst cited by CommonWealth Magazine. That’s not a roadmap gap or a yield problem—it’s a physical constraint in the current platform.

TSMC executives had previously insisted that 9.5 reticles was the CoWoS ceiling, with panel-level packaging meant to take over beyond that threshold. Analysts now read TSMC’s subsequent maneuvering as a defensive response to Intel’s EMIB gaining traction. Under TSMC’s original roadmap, its next-generation panel-level packaging technology, CoPoS, wouldn’t reach volume production until after 2029—leaving a gap between its current 9.5-reticle CoWoS maximum and ultra-large SoW packaging at over 40 reticles. “TSMC assumed customers wouldn’t leave—but they did,” as one analyst put it.

This matters beyond the Google-MediaTek relationship. It means that as AI chips grow denser and more complex—combining compute dies, I/O dies, and HBM in a single package—the reticle ceiling becomes a genuine engineering wall, not just a procurement inconvenience.

What EMIB Actually Is (and Isn’t)

Intel’s Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge is not a CoWoS replacement. That distinction is worth being precise about, because the two are regularly compared as if they were competing for the same workload.

EMIB skips the interposer entirely. Instead of mounting dies on a large silicon interposer, small silicon bridges are embedded directly into the organic substrate of the package—die-to-die connections happen through these embedded bridges, not through a full-coverage silicon layer beneath the chips. The structural difference is significant: two layers (dies and substrate) instead of three (dies, interposer, substrate).

Intel’s EMIB offers several advantages over TSMC’s CoWoS: it eliminates the need for a large interposer through embedded silicon bridges, improves manufacturing yield, reduces warpage risk from CTE mismatches between different materials, and enhances long-term reliability.

The scaling numbers are striking. EMIB-M already reaches 6× reticle scaling and is projected to achieve 8–12× by 2026–2027, compared to CoWoS-S at 3.3× and CoWoS-L at around 3.5× today. That gap is why HumuFish can’t run on CoWoS but plausibly can run on EMIB.

The tradeoff, though, is real. The silicon bridge’s smaller area and routing density constrain bandwidth, increase transmission distance, and result in slightly higher latency than CoWoS. As a result, EMIB is seen as more attractive to ASIC customers than GPU vendors, which require extremely high bandwidth and ultra-low latency.

This is the nuance that gets lost in coverage that frames EMIB as simply “Intel’s CoWoS.” NVIDIA will not switch its GPU roadmap to EMIB. The latency and bandwidth requirements for training at scale, where NVIDIA’s hardware dominates, favor CoWoS’s interposer architecture. But inference ASICs, custom accelerators, and application-specific chips with less aggressive bandwidth requirements are a different story.

MediaTek’s Split and What It Signals

MediaTek is reportedly involved in the core design of the training-focused TPU v8t, which uses TSMC’s N3P process and CoWoS-S advanced packaging, while the TPU v8e is expected to adopt Intel’s EMIB technology. So even within a single generation, different TPU variants are heading to different packaging platforms.

The TPU v8x (codenamed Zebrafish) is expected to enter mass production in July–August 2026, with shipment estimates of 400,000 units in 2026 and between 1.5 and 2.0 million units in 2027. The next-generation v9 (codenamed Humufish) is expected to pivot to Intel’s EMIB packaging to alleviate CoWoS capacity bottlenecks and accommodate its larger die configuration.

The financial stakes are correspondingly large. Analysts estimate that AI ASIC revenue will jump from 8% of MediaTek’s total in 2026 to 27% in 2027, with ASIC projected to surpass mobile chips as the company’s largest growth driver within three years.

Industry analysts believe the dual packaging strategy could help MediaTek secure greater priority access to CoWoS capacity while positioning the company ahead of its projected AI ASIC shipments in 2028, when its market share is estimated to reach 26%, or nearly 5 million units.

The hire of Douglas Yu—a former TSMC R&D and advanced packaging executive—as a part-time advisor signals how seriously MediaTek is treating this transition. According to reporting from The Journalist, the move is intended not only to deepen cooperation with TSMC on CoWoS and SoIC, but also to strengthen MediaTek’s capabilities in next-generation ASIC and AI infrastructure packaging technologies. Recruiting the person who helped build CoWoS while simultaneously expanding into EMIB is a particular kind of institutional ambidexterity.

The Geopolitical Layer

There’s a dimension to Google’s packaging shift that has nothing to do with reticle sizes.

According to a source cited by CommonWealth Magazine, Google’s move toward Intel’s EMIB packaging also reflects a geopolitical consideration—aligning with a non-China, non-Taiwan (NCNT) sourcing strategy. Intel’s packaging operations are based in the United States, specifically in New Mexico and Oregon. For a hyperscaler trying to demonstrate supply chain resilience to regulators and investors, that geography matters independently of the technical merits.

This is not unique to Google. EE Times reported that Intel Foundry VP of Packaging and Test Mark Gardner noted that much of the advanced packaging shortage stems from customers locked into competitor technologies, adding that Intel holds an advantage because it isn’t constrained by capacity—and that AWS and MediaTek are among chip designers tapping Intel Foundry as a packaging supplier.

The NCNT framing is worth watching closely. If it becomes a standard procurement filter for large US-based cloud providers—and there are policy reasons it might—then Intel’s domestic packaging capacity becomes a strategic moat that has very little to do with EMIB’s technical specifications.

The Yield Problem Intel Hasn’t Solved Yet

The honest counterpoint to EMIB’s momentum is that the technology is not yet proven at mass-production scale for these workloads.

According to analyst Ming-Chi Kuo, Intel’s EMIB-T advanced packaging technology has reached approximately 90% yield in technical verification—but the production standard benchmarked against FCBGA is 98%, and the leap from 90% to 98% is historically the most difficult phase of yield improvement.

TSMC’s own CoWoS packaging yield target for 2026 starts at 98%. This implies essentially no margin for compromise on EMIB yield expectations from Google. Kuo described himself as cautious about EMIB meeting mass production standards smoothly in the near-to-medium term.

That’s not a reason to dismiss EMIB’s trajectory. Supply chain sources say MediaTek sees significant value in EMIB, with execution progress reportedly advancing smoothly. But the 90% figure is a reminder that “promising technology adopted by a major customer” and “technology ready to ship 1.5 million units a year” are meaningfully different statements.

Broader Industry Ripple Effects

MediaTek isn’t alone in exploring this path. SK Hynix is reportedly conducting R&D on Intel’s EMIB-based 2.5D packaging technology using its own HBM, testing the integration of HBM and logic semiconductors using EMIB-integrated substrates supplied by Intel. Marvell is also reportedly reviewing potential EMIB adoption.

TrendForce notes that EMIB is poised to drive significant growth for Intel Foundry Services, as Google plans to adopt the technology in its 2027 TPU v9 and Meta is considering it for MTIA accelerators.

The pattern emerging here is a deliberate, if still nascent, diversification of the AI chip packaging supply chain away from single-source dependency on TSMC’s CoWoS. It’s not a crisis or a competitive rout—TSMC’s technology remains technically superior for high-bandwidth, latency-sensitive workloads. But the combination of capacity constraints, reticle size limits, geopolitical pressure, and Intel’s improving yield numbers has created enough oxygen for a real alternative to take shape.

What This Means Going Forward

For anyone watching the AI infrastructure supply chain, a few things are worth tracking specifically.

First, the CoWoS ceiling will become more visible as AI chip designs keep growing. NVIDIA’s Rubin architecture, AMD’s next MI-series parts, and Google’s own TPU roadmap all point toward larger, more complex packages. The more chips bump against the 9.5-reticle limit, the more EMIB’s scaling advantage becomes practically relevant rather than theoretically interesting.

Second, Intel’s yield trajectory over the next 12 months matters enormously. Getting from 90% to 98% will either validate the EMIB narrative or put a hard ceiling on how quickly the technology can scale into high-volume production. That’s not something you can assess from roadmap slides—it’ll show up in supply chain sourcing decisions.

Third, MediaTek’s transformation from mobile chip company to AI ASIC supplier is happening faster than most of the industry expected. Running two advanced packaging platforms simultaneously—one mature, one still proving itself—is the kind of operational complexity that either forces discipline or creates failure modes. Which outcome materializes will depend on engineering execution that isn’t visible yet.

The dual packaging strategy is, at its core, a bet that the AI chip market is growing fast enough that no single packaging technology can serve all of it. That bet looks well-placed. The question is whether the execution holds up when the volumes get real.
