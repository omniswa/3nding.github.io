ICANN's New gTLD Round Is Open: What It Means

May 3, 2026 7-Minute Read

The last time ICANN opened the door for new generic top-level domains, smartphones were relatively new, cloud computing was still a buzzword, and Amazon hadn’t yet become a logistics company. That was 2012. The result was over 1,200 new extensions—from .app and .shop to .london and .google—and a fundamental shift in how organizations could present themselves online. [CentralNic Registry] Then the window closed.

On April 30, 2026, ICANN opened applications again [PR Newswire] , and the landscape is meaningfully different from what it was fourteen years ago.

What ICANN Is Actually Offering

A gTLD is the string after the final dot in a web address. Most people interact with .com, .org, or .net daily without thinking about it. What ICANN’s program allows is for an organization to operate one of these—to become the registry that controls a top-level namespace entirely.

For the first time in over a decade, businesses, communities, governments, and other organizations have the opportunity to operate unique digital assets such as .brand, .city, or .industry. [PR Newswire]

Three broad categories dominate the applications:

  • Brand TLDs — Companies controlling their own digital namespace. .google and .coca-cola were both secured in the 2012 round. [Rouse]
  • Geographic extensions — Cities and regions carving out distinct internet identities. .berlin and .nyc emerged from the last round.
  • Generic strings — Open commercial extensions like .shop or .wiki that any registrar can offer to the public.

These aren’t the same as buying a domain name. This is applying to operate a top-level domain registry—internet infrastructure with contractual, technical, financial, and compliance obligations. [Newgtldprogram]

The Timeline and What Happens Next

The application submission period opened on April 30, 2026 and will close on August 12, 2026. [ICANN] That’s 105 days, which sounds generous until you factor in the internal approvals, technical documentation, and registry service provider arrangements most applicants need to finalize beforehand.

After the window closes, the process doesn’t speed up. Approximately two months later, ICANN publishes a public list of all TLDs and applicants that passed initial checks—called Reveal Day—where contention sets for identical string applications are also published. [CentralNic Registry]

One new mechanism this round: a 14-day Replacement Period follows Reveal Day, during which applicants who submitted a backup string can swap to it if their primary string is contested. [CentralNic Registry] This is an attempt to reduce expensive head-to-head disputes before formal evaluation begins.

Based on the 2012 experience—where the application window closed in April 2012 and the first Registry Agreement wasn’t signed until roughly 18 months later—the realistic expectation for any delegated TLDs is 2027 at the earliest, likely later for contested strings. [CSC]

The Cost Structure Is More Complex Than One Number

The headline figure is $227,000. ICANN has set an expected evaluation fee of USD 227,000 per application [ICANNWiki] , up from $185,000 in 2012. But that’s not the whole picture.

Auction costs in the 2012 round—particularly in competitive commercial categories like .app and .shop—led to multimillion-dollar bids that far exceeded original application budgets. [Dn] Organizations targeting any string with obvious commercial appeal should budget for this possibility from the start.

An applicant that withdraws after passing Initial Evaluation may receive only a partial refund, whereas an early-stage withdrawal could preserve a larger portion of the fee. [Dn] The point: timing a withdrawal matters, and it introduces a risk-based decision that most applicants underestimate going in.

For brand TLD applicants specifically—typically large corporations applying for their own name—the math is often simpler. The $227,000 is a one-time cost to control an entire namespace indefinitely. Measured against annual brand protection and cybersecurity spending, many legal teams consider it reasonable. The complication is ongoing operations, which are not covered by the application fee.

What Changed Since 2012

The 2026 round isn’t just a repeat. Several structural improvements address failures from the last cycle.

Private contention resolution is banned

In 2012, competing applicants for the same string could settle privately. This created opaque deals, occasional accusations of anti-competitive behavior, and unpredictable outcomes. The 2026 round introduces an explicit ban on private contention resolution. [CentralNic Registry]

The applicant support program is substantively different

The redesigned Applicant Support Program has been a major success compared to the 2012 round, which received only 3 ASP applications. [Domaindetails] The program is intended to lower barriers for organizations from developing economies and underrepresented communities that were largely absent from the first round.

The non-Latin internet gets a real expansion

The 2026 Round accepts gTLD applications in 27 different scripts, representing hundreds of languages such as Arabic, Chinese, Devanagari, and Thai. [PR Newswire] In 2012, IDN coverage was uneven and the rules were inconsistent. IDN applications for TLDs in non-ASCII scripts now receive priority processing, and Label Generation Rules are available for all 27 supported scripts. [CentralNic Registry]

This matters beyond the symbolism. A large share of internet users primarily read and write in scripts that have been systematically underserved by a DNS built around ASCII. Expanding the root zone in this direction isn’t just an inclusivity measure—it changes which communities can build native digital infrastructure.

Closed generics are off the table (for now)

One controversial aspect of the 2012 round was the prospect of “closed generic” gTLDs—where a company applies for a common word (.book, .cloud, .search) and restricts registrations exclusively to itself. Amazon’s bid for .book drew significant backlash. The ICANN Board decided in January 2024 that closed generic gTLD applications will not be permitted in the 2026 round unless and until an approved framework is developed to assess their compatibility with the public interest. [ICANNWiki]

Who Should Actually Be Paying Attention

Not every organization that could apply should apply. A few categories where the case is clearer:

Large consumer brands with active phishing and cybersquatting problems. Controlling .yourbrand means controlling the entire namespace—every subdomain, every certificate, every email address. The security argument alone often justifies the cost for companies managing active brand abuse.

Cities and regional governments that want a permanent, governable digital presence. .nyc has issued over a million domains. The model works when a city has the municipal will to operate or contract an actual registry.

Organizations targeting non-Latin markets. The 2026 expansion of IDN scripts opens territory that didn’t meaningfully exist in 2012. A registry operating a domain suffix in Arabic or Devanagari has essentially no incumbent competition at the TLD level.

Registry entrepreneurs who missed 2012 and have been watching the market. The process ends approximately around 2030. [The Register] There won’t be another window for years.

The One Risk Nobody Talks About Enough

Cybersquatters move faster than ICANN does. Once new extensions become available for general domain registrations, third parties may register names incorporating your brand—potentially before you do. [Rouse]

This is the defensive argument for monitoring, even for organizations that have no intention of applying. When new gTLDs begin delegating—likely in 2027 and beyond—the initial sunrise and landrush periods will be chaotic. Brand owners who haven’t mapped their exposure in advance consistently lose ground during these windows.

The 2012 round proved this. Companies that tracked applications and engaged during objection periods came out better than those that waited for delegation to arrive before acting.

The Bigger Picture

The domain name system as we know it emerged from RFC 920, written by internet pioneers Jon Postel and Joyce Reynolds, which proposed creating .gov, .edu, .com, .mil, and .org. [The Register] That was the 1980s. What ICANN is doing in 2026 is a continuation of the same underlying logic: the internet outgrows its naming conventions, and the root zone expands to accommodate new uses.

The difference now is that the organizations applying aren’t just registrars speculating on commercial extensions. They’re cities, universities, global brands, and communities in languages that the original DNS never anticipated. That’s a meaningfully different expansion than what 2012 produced.

Whether any given organization applies is a financial and strategic question with no universal answer. But the window is open, the rules are clearer than they were fourteen years ago, and August 12th is the hard deadline.
