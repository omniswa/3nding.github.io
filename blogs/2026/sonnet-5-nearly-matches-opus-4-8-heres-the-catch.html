Sonnet 5 Nearly Matches Opus 4.8. Here's the Catch.

July 1, 2026 9-Minute Read

Most people assume a mid-tier model exists to be a cheaper, weaker version of the flagship. Sonnet 5 breaks that assumption in one specific place: on a knowledge-work benchmark called GDPval-AA v2, it scores 1,618 against Opus 4.8’s 1,615. That’s the first time a Sonnet has beaten its concurrent Opus in Anthropic’s own testing, and it happened because Anthropic stopped treating Sonnet as a stripped-down Opus and started tuning it specifically for sustained, multi-step task execution.

If you’re deciding which model to point your API traffic at today, here’s the direct answer. Use Sonnet 5 for almost everything: coding, agent workflows, research, document analysis, customer-facing chat. It launches at an introductory price of $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens through August 31, 2026, after which it rises to $3 and $15. Keep Opus 4.8 in reserve for the handful of tasks where the last few points of accuracy actually change the outcome. That’s it. The rest of this piece explains why that split makes sense and where it doesn’t.

The Benchmark Gap Shrank Because Anthropic Stopped Optimizing Sonnet for Single-Turn Answers

Older Sonnet models were built to answer well. Sonnet 5 is built to keep working. That distinction explains almost every number in the release.

On SWE-bench Pro, a test of realistic agentic coding tasks, Sonnet 5 scores 63.2%, up from Sonnet 4.6’s showing and within striking distance of Opus 4.8. Opus 4.8 still leads that particular test at 69.2%, a six-point gap that matters if you’re shipping code without a human review step. But on Terminal-Bench 2.1, which measures how well a model handles messy command-line workflows across many steps, Sonnet 5 jumps to 80.4%, a huge climb from Sonnet 4.6’s 67.0%, and it’s close enough to Opus 4.8’s own terminal score that the difference stops being the deciding factor for most teams.

Here’s the mechanism behind that jump. A terminal task isn’t one inference call. It’s dozens of small decisions strung together: run a command, read the output, decide whether it worked, adjust, try again. A model that’s slightly worse at any single decision compounds that weakness across forty steps. A model that’s built to self-correct — to notice a failed step and recover instead of drifting — closes that gap even without a corresponding jump in raw intelligence. That’s the specific thing Anthropic tuned for in this release, and it shows up more in multi-step benchmarks than in single-shot reasoning tests.

The place where Sonnet 5 actually overtakes Opus is telling for the same reason. GDPval-AA v2 measures applied knowledge work — document analysis, information synthesis, producing structured outputs — the kind of task that’s long and iterative but doesn’t require the deepest possible reasoning at any single step. That’s exactly the profile Sonnet 5 was built to win.

Where the gap stays wide is anywhere raw, unaided reasoning is the whole test. Without tool access, Opus 4.8 scores 49.8% on Humanity’s Last Exam against Sonnet 5’s 43.2%, a 6.6-point spread. Take tools away and the newer, cheaper model can’t compensate the way it does elsewhere. That’s your practical filter: if the task lets a model check its own work — run code, search, re-read a file — Sonnet 5 competes. If it’s a closed-book reasoning problem, Opus still wins clearly.

Why the Price Actually Matters More Than the Benchmarks Do

A six-point benchmark gap sounds significant until you put a dollar figure next to it. An output-heavy agent workload running at $1,000 a day on Opus 4.8 lands closer to $400 a day on Sonnet 5 at standard pricing. That’s not a rounding error. For a team running hundreds of parallel agents, it’s the line between a project that ships and one that gets killed in a budget review.

The mechanism here is straightforward. Anthropic prices by the token, both in and out. Sonnet 5’s standard rate of $3 input and $15 output sits well under Opus 4.8’s $5 and $25. Multiply that difference across millions of tokens a day and the gap stops being about model quality and starts being about whether the product is financially viable at all.

The introductory pricing sweetens this further, but not by accident. A spokesperson told The New Stack the discount exists so customers can test Sonnet 5 against their real workloads at the lowest possible cost during the migration window. In plain terms: Anthropic wants developers to actually run their production prompts through the new model before the price goes up, because that’s the only way anyone finds out if the accuracy trade-off is acceptable for their specific use case. Benchmarks are averages. Your workload is not an average.

There’s a wrinkle worth knowing before you run that test, though. Sonnet 5 uses an updated tokenizer — the same one introduced with Opus 4.7 — and the same input text can map to roughly 1.0 to 1.35 times more tokens than it did under the old system. So a prompt that cost you X tokens on Sonnet 4.6 might cost 1.2X on Sonnet 5. The lower per-token price mostly absorbs that, but it means a naive “tokens times price” comparison against your old bill will understate what you’re actually spending. Measure the real invoice, not the sticker price.

Effort Levels Are Where the Real Decision Happens, Not the Model Name

Sonnet 5 doesn’t behave like one model. It behaves like four, depending on how much reasoning budget you give it: low, medium, high, and xhigh. This is the part most comparisons skip, and it’s the part that actually determines whether switching from Opus makes sense.

At low and medium effort, Sonnet 5 is the clear value pick — cheap, fast, and close enough to Opus on most tasks that the gap doesn’t justify the price. Push it to xhigh, though, and the math flips. At its maxed-out reasoning setting, Sonnet 5 performs roughly in line with Opus 4.8’s medium-to-high setting on computer-use and agentic search benchmarks — but running Sonnet at that ceiling burns enough extra tokens that it can cost more than just using Opus 4.8 directly at a comparable setting.

Why does turning up the effort dial cost so much more than the benchmark gain implies? Because effort level controls how many reasoning tokens the model spends thinking before it answers, and that spend scales faster than the accuracy improvement does. You’re paying for diminishing returns on the same curve every reasoning model follows: the last few points of quality are always the most expensive ones to buy. Anthropic’s own point here is that the two models now sit on one continuous cost-performance curve instead of two separate tiers — you can dial Sonnet up or Opus down and land in overlapping territory. The practical move is to default to Sonnet at low or medium effort for routine work, and escalate specific hard tasks to Opus rather than cranking Sonnet’s effort dial and hoping it catches up.

The Safety Numbers Are Better, But Not the Whole Story

Sonnet 5 shipped with real-time cyber safeguards, the same detection system used in Opus 4.7 and 4.8, turned on from day one rather than added later. That’s a change from how some past releases handled it. Anthropic’s safety assessments found Sonnet 5 shows an overall lower rate of undesirable behaviors than Sonnet 4.6 and is generally safer to use in agentic contexts.

But lower than its predecessor isn’t the same as lowest in the lineup. Sonnet 5 still shows higher rates of misaligned behavior than the more capable Opus 4.8, and Anthropic explicitly points security researchers who need reduced guardrails toward Opus 4.8 instead, since Sonnet 5 wasn’t deliberately trained for cybersecurity tasks the way Opus was. If your workflow touches penetration testing, exploit research, or anything adjacent to offensive security, this isn’t a cost decision. Use Opus.

What “Cheap Until August” Actually Means for Your Roadmap

The intro pricing has a hard expiration date, and that date should shape your migration plan, not just your curiosity. Anthropic isn’t running a permanent discount — it’s running a six-week window meant to get real production traffic onto the model before the price normalizes on September 1.

That means the smart sequence looks like this: pilot Sonnet 5 now, on your actual prompts, while the per-token cost is at its lowest. Measure two things, not one — accuracy against your existing Opus or Sonnet 4.6 baseline, and total cost including the tokenizer’s slightly higher token count per request. If the numbers hold up during the discount window, you’ve de-risked the switch before the price even goes up. If they don’t, you’ve lost six weeks of cheap testing, not six weeks of committed infrastructure.

One more factor worth naming plainly, since it affects how much weight to put on “Sonnet 5 is basically Opus now.” Anthropic’s top tier, Mythos 5 and Fable 5, isn’t part of this comparison at all right now — both have been offline since June 12, 2026 under a US export-control directive with no confirmed return date. So the ceiling of what Claude can do publicly right now sits at Opus 4.8, and Sonnet 5’s job is to get as close to that ceiling as possible for as little money as possible. It does that job well. It doesn’t change what that ceiling is.

The Bottom Line

Sonnet 5 doesn’t beat Opus 4.8. It makes beating Opus 4.8 unnecessary for most of what people actually do with Claude day to day. The two models now sit on one shared cost-performance curve instead of two disconnected tiers, which means the real decision isn’t “which model” anymore — it’s “how much effort, on which task, for how much money.” Set your default to Sonnet 5 at low or medium effort, reserve Opus for hard coding, deep reasoning, and cybersecurity work, and run your own numbers before August 31 while the discount is still live.
