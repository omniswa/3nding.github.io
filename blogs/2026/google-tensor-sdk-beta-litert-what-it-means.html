Google Tensor SDK Beta + LiteRT: What It Means

May 21, 2026 8-Minute Read

Most “on-device AI” announcements are really just “slightly smaller cloud AI.” What Google released on May 19, 2026 is different in a specific way: the Tensor ML SDK Beta gives third-party developers direct, structured access to the same TPU hardware that powers Pixel’s own features—Pro Zoom, Voice Translate, Add Me, Call Notes. That gap between what Google’s own apps could do on-device and what the developer ecosystem could do has existed since the first Tensor chip. The Beta closes it, at least on the Pixel 10 family, and the integration with LiteRT is the part that makes it actually usable.

What Changed Going from EAP to Beta

The Tensor ML SDK wasn’t new when this dropped. It had been in an Experimental Access Program, which is Google’s way of saying “we’ll give you early access if you sign up and accept that things will break.” Beta means two concrete things changed: the workflow stabilized around LiteRT, and the model garden reached a scale—100+ models—that makes it useful without requiring you to bring your own everything.

The SDK is now integrated with LiteRT, offering a workflow to convert, compile, deploy, and run ML and generative AI models on Pixel via Tensor’s TPU. The Pixel 10 devices supported are the standard Pixel 10, Pixel 10 Pro, Pixel 10 Pro XL, and Pixel 10 Pro Fold—all running the Tensor G5 chip, which moved to TSMC fabrication from the G1–G4’s Samsung process.

The workflow has three stages, and each one has a practical decision point buried inside it.

The Compile → Deploy → Run Pipeline

Compilation is where you start. You convert and compile PyTorch or TFLite models into optimized binaries using LiteRT Torch. LiteRT itself—renamed from TensorFlow Lite in September 2024—was rebranded to signal its expansion beyond TensorFlow models. Since its debut in 2017, TFLite enabled ML experiences in over 100K apps running on 2.7B devices, and the name LiteRT captures a multi-framework vision: start in any popular framework, run on-device with strong performance.

Here’s the thing about compilation that the announcement glosses over: the Google Tensor SDK Beta doesn’t yet support on-device (JIT) compilation—only AOT. That distinction matters. AOT (ahead-of-time) means you compile your model for the Tensor TPU before shipping it, which you then bundle into your app or deliver via AI Pack. JIT (just-in-time) would let the device compile on first run, which is how Qualcomm and MediaTek paths currently work in LiteRT. AOT is actually the better path for performance—it eliminates initialization overhead and reduces memory usage when the user first launches your app—but it also means you’re committing to known target SoCs at build time. For an app targeting exclusively the Pixel 10 family, that’s not a problem. For a general Android app with Pixel 10 as one of many targets, you need a separate compilation artifact.

Deployment uses Play Feature Delivery and AI Packs. Play Feature Delivery distributes and installs the compatible runtime and compiler libraries that connect to the on-device TPU drivers, while AI Packs bundle and deliver the compiled model files within your application. The practical implication is that your app doesn’t ship with everything pre-bundled—the runtime libraries arrive at install time or on-demand, which keeps initial app size manageable but adds a dependency on Play infrastructure. Apps not distributed through the Play Store have a harder path here.

Inference is where it gets pleasant. LiteRT Runtime runs your model on the TPU with just a few lines of code and allows you to enable fallback mechanisms by specifying CPU or GPU as secondary options, automatically using them depending on TPU availability. The fallback isn’t just a convenience feature—it’s structural. If a particular model operator isn’t supported by the Tensor TPU kernel, the runtime can route that subgraph to the GPU or CPU transparently. Whether that happens silently or gives you observable hooks depends on your logging setup, which is worth checking before you assume everything hit the TPU.

What the Model Garden Actually Offers

The model garden provides over 100 classic ML models and generative AI models including Gemma 3 1B, alongside precompiled models available to download from the LiteRT Hugging Face community.

The precompiled part is underappreciated. Compilation for a specific TPU is not a five-minute process—it requires the LiteRT toolchain, a working environment, and iteration time. Having Google-compiled, Tensor-TPU-optimized binaries available for download means you can prototype an image segmentation or speech recognition feature in a day rather than spending it on toolchain setup.

The generative AI side of the garden includes a few models worth understanding individually:

Function Gemma (270M parameters, fine-tuned for mobile actions) is designed for local function calling—triggering in-app actions from natural language without a network round trip. At 270M parameters running on the Tensor TPU, it’s fast enough to be plausibly interactive.

EmbeddingGemma (300M parameters) produces semantic embeddings locally. This is the piece that enables semantic search, similarity matching, and RAG-style retrieval within an app without sending text off-device. That’s a real privacy win for anything handling user documents or messages.

The vision models cover object detection, depth mapping, and body tracking. For audio, you get end-to-end speech recognition for secure, low-latency transcription, voice-controlled accessibility tools, and translation on the edge.

How LiteRT Sits Across the NPU Ecosystem

LiteRT isn’t just a Pixel/Tensor abstraction—it’s Google’s unified layer across Google Tensor, Qualcomm AI Engine Direct, MediaTek NeuroPilot, and Intel OpenVINO. The Tensor SDK Beta is one slice of that. Understanding the broader picture clarifies some of the design decisions.

LiteRT addresses NPU fragmentation challenges by providing a unified, simplified deployment workflow that abstracts away low-level, vendor-specific SDKs and handles variation across numerous SoC variants. The performance ceiling is significant: NPU acceleration with LiteRT delivers up to 100x speedup compared to CPU execution and 10x speedup over GPU processing.

Real-world numbers from production deployments are more grounded. Argmax, which built an on-device speech recognition SDK using LiteRT and AI Pack delivery, found that upgrading from GPU to NPU delivers over 2x speedup across Google Tensor, MediaTek, and Qualcomm SoCs. The 100x figure represents a ceiling under ideal conditions; 2x over GPU in a shipping app handling a large speech model is a more representative outcome.

The comparison across vendors is worth a closer look. Qualcomm and MediaTek paths support both AOT and JIT compilation through the CompiledModel API. Google Tensor supports AOT execution through the CompiledModel API—on-device JIT compilation is still listed as Beta-not-yet-supported. That puts Tensor behind Qualcomm and MediaTek in deployment flexibility, even though Tensor’s TPU is purpose-built for exactly this workload. The reason is likely that JIT requires distributing the compiler itself to the device, which has size, security, and stability implications. Google may be taking a more conservative path here, or JIT support may be coming in a subsequent Beta update.

The Tradeoff No One Mentions

The entire Tensor SDK approach—AOT-compiled binaries, Play-distributed runtimes, curated model garden—optimizes for performance and reliability at the cost of target flexibility.

If you’re building a Pixel-exclusive app or a feature that gracefully degrades on non-Pixel hardware, this workflow is excellent. If you’re building a cross-Android ML feature and want Pixel to be one of many acceleration targets, you’ll maintain separate compilation pipelines per SoC family. LiteRT’s unified API helps at the inference call site, but the build and distribution side remains fragmented by hardware vendor.

The model garden also has an implicit constraint: it’s curated by Google, which means it reflects Google’s model choices. If your production model is a fine-tuned variant of something in the garden, you’re bringing a custom artifact through the compilation pipeline. If you’ve built something novel—a custom transformer architecture, a quantized model from a non-standard training setup—you need to validate that LiteRT Torch can convert it cleanly, and that the resulting kernels actually hit the TPU rather than falling back silently.

That said, the fallback mechanism is genuinely well-designed. You can specify CPU, GPU, or both as options, and LiteRT will automatically use them if the NPU is unavailable—including support for fallback in AOT-compiled artifacts. This means a single app binary can deliver hardware-accelerated inference on Pixel 10 and acceptable CPU-based inference everywhere else, without you writing hardware detection logic.

Getting Started

Documentation lives at [ai.google.dev/edge/tensor-sdk], and the model garden is at [ai.google.dev/edge/tensor-sdk/model-garden]. Precompiled models for the Tensor TPU are on the LiteRT Hugging Face community under the litert-community/google-tensor collection.

Google released a codelab through I/O 2026 that walks through model compilation and deployment to a Pixel 10 device, which is the fastest path to a working end-to-end build. The sample app covers image segmentation using the CompiledModel API and is a reasonable starting point for understanding the AOT compilation output format.

The practical starting checklist:

  • Confirm your model converts cleanly through LiteRT Torch (PyTorch) or the existing TFLite converter path
  • Run the compiled binary through the Tensor SDK’s profiling tools to verify TPU delegation before shipping
  • Set explicit fallback targets so you know what happens on Pixel 9 or non-Pixel devices
  • Use AI Packs for model delivery, not bundling—anything large enough to warrant TPU acceleration is too large to ship inside an APK

The Beta designation is appropriate. This is functional, the model garden is genuinely useful, and the LiteRT integration eliminates most of the low-level complexity that made earlier Tensor SDK access painful. The JIT compilation gap and the Play distribution dependency are real constraints. Neither is a dealbreaker for apps where Pixel is a primary target—they’re just things to account for before you commit to the workflow.
